FormulaCelesteThe F1 Engineering Challenge · New for 2026

Design a 20-metre race car on paper, package it in CAD, prove it in CFD, then defend every gram and every millisecond in front of motorsport engineers.

Formula Celeste is the headline competition of CelesteCon 2026, open to school teams across India in two divisions, Grades 6–8 and Grades 9–12. Every team founds a racing constructor, engineers a scale Formula 1 car powered by a single compressed-gas cartridge and guided by a tether line down a 20.0 m straight, and runs that constructor as a business: identity, sponsors, a budget cap and a risk register.

In Round 1, teams submit an Engineering & Enterprise Dossier with a complete CAD and CFD package as a single PDF on the CelesteCon portal. Up to 12 shortlisted teams per division then bring their physical car to DPS R.K. Section 01 · Premise

The brief: speed is never an accident

In Formula 1, a lap time is not luck, courage or styling. It is the mathematical consequence of downforce balanced against drag, of mass placed where it helps rather than where it happened to fit, of friction hunted down one bearing at a time, and of decisions made in milliseconds on the pit wall by people who already know what the numbers say.

Formula Celeste brings that discipline to school engineering. Your car is small, around 200 mm long and about 50 grams, but the physics is the same physics. A compressed-gas cartridge delivers roughly 22 newtons of thrust for a fraction of a second, accelerating the car at more than 25 g. For the rest of the run, everything you designed is either keeping that speed or spending it. Your job is to know, in writing and before race day, exactly which is which.

A fast car that nobody can explain loses to a slightly slower car whose team can defend every gram, every millimetre and every millisecond.

What your team actually builds

Every team founds a racing constructor: a named team with an identity, roles, a budget and sponsors. The constructor designs one car to its division's Technical Regulations, models it as a fully dimensioned 3D assembly, analyses its aerodynamics with a real CFD solver (Senior) or a documented flow study (Junior), plans how every part will be made, and predicts how fast it will cover 20.000 m before it has ever run.

The dossier is judged first, blind. Shortlisted constructors then bring the physical car to DPS R.K. Puram, where it is weighed and measured against the regulations, run on the CelesteCon track, and defended in front of a jury of motorsport and mechanical engineers.

Why a straight, tethered track changes everything

These cars never corner. A tether line runs through two guides under the car and keeps it on the lane, so aerodynamic downforce does not buy cornering grip the way it does on a real circuit. What it does do is press the car onto the track, which raises rolling resistance, and generate induced drag. What it also does is keep a 30:1 thrust-to-weight machine from pitching, lifting its front wheels or binding on the tether. Every team must decide how much downforce the car needs, and prove the number. Teams that claim downforce "makes the car faster" without a force budget will lose marks in every round.

What the jury will check first

  • That the car on the scales is the car in the CAD, to within the tolerances in the regulations.
  • That the drag coefficient has a traceable source: a CFD case with a mesh study, a coast-down test, or a documented build-up.
  • That the thrust line sits at or above the centre of gravity, and that the team knows why it must.
  • That the predicted 20 m time comes from a force model, and that the team can explain the difference between prediction and measurement.
  • That the sponsorship plan offers a sponsor something measurable in return for its money.
T1

The aerodynamics & downforce test

Can your team size a front and rear wing, shape the underbody, and state the car's drag and downforce as numbers with sources?

  • Wing span, chord, incidence and camber chosen for a Reynolds number near 104, not copied from full-size cars.
  • Venturi underbody channels (Senior) with a stated inlet-to-throat area ratio and suction pressure.
  • Total CD, CL and CL/CD at the design speed, with an aero balance split front to rear.

Fails if: CD appears with no method behind it, or downforce is claimed to make a tethered straight-line car faster.

T2

The scrutineering & bounding-box test

Does the car obey every dimension, exclusion zone and mass limit, and can you prove it on paper before the scrutineer proves it with callipers?

  • Length 170–210 mm, width ≤ 85 mm, height ≤ 65 mm, wheel Ø 26–34 mm.
  • Minimum mass without cartridge: Junior 55.0 g, Senior 50.00 g, strict.
  • Tether channel, wheel clear-view zones, cockpit envelope and launch-striker zone kept clear.

Fails if: any regulated dimension on the car cannot be found on drawing F1-CD-1 or F1-CD-3.

T3

The launch kinematics & friction test

Can your team turn a cartridge thrust curve into a speed trace and a 20 m time, with every loss accounted for?

  • Thrust from gas mass flow, F = ṁ·vₑ, and total impulse J = ∫F dt.
  • Peak acceleration above 25 g, peak speed, and the time at which drag overtakes thrust.
  • Wheel rotational inertia, bearing friction and tether-guide drag as named losses.

Fails if: the predicted time has no force model behind it, or friction and rotating mass are silently ignored.

T4

The cross-examination & enterprise test

Can the team defend its decisions live, and run the constructor as a business?

  • CNC toolpath choices, 3D-printing orientation and surface finish, explained by the team that made them.
  • CFD mesh convergence and the difference between a colourful picture and a converged result.
  • Centre of pressure behind centre of gravity, and a sponsorship return a sponsor would sign.

Fails if: the team cannot open its own CAD feature tree, or cannot say what a sponsor gets for its money.

1.1What this is, and what it is not

Not this

  • A styling contest judged on paint, decals and how "aggressive" the car looks.
  • Words such as "sleek", "futuristic" or "aerodynamic" standing in for numbers.
  • Rainbow CFD pictures with no mesh, no boundary conditions and no convergence history.
  • A top speed in km/h with no thrust curve, drag estimate or friction model behind it.
  • A car bought, printed or machined by someone else and explained by the team afterwards.
  • A sponsorship section that lists logos but not what each sponsor receives.

This

  • A racing constructor with a car, a budget, a sponsor plan and a risk register.
  • A force budget that accounts for thrust, drag, downforce, rolling resistance and rotating mass.
  • Drawings dimensioned to 0.1 mm that a scrutineer can check the real car against.
  • CFD (Senior) with a mesh study, stated boundary conditions and results that match hand estimates.
  • A predicted 20 m time, a measured time, and a team that can explain the difference.
  • A car the team designed, modelled and manufactured itself, with every tool declared.

1.3What wins at Formula Celeste

A

Aerodynamic efficiency

The least drag for the downforce the car genuinely needs, with every coefficient sourced and the aero balance known front to rear.

Judge asks: "What is your CD, and how do you know?"

B

Precision CAD/CAM execution

A model that is the car: parametric, dimensioned, compliant, and manufactured by a process the team chose, set up and can justify.

Judge asks: "Show me the feature that sets your chamber wall thickness."

C

Defensible mathematics

A calculations ledger in which every figure has an input, a method, a result and a check, and in which the predicted time follows from the forces.

Judge asks: "Your car ran 38 ms slower than predicted. Where did the time go?"

D

Enterprise acumen

A constructor that raises money honestly, spends it where it buys the most time, and gives its sponsors a measurable return.

Judge asks: "Which rupee on your budget bought the most milliseconds?"

1.4The constructor's budget cap

Junior cap · ₹12,000

Total spend on the car, spares, tooling consumables, pit display and printed materials. Travel, food and accommodation for Round 2 are excluded.

Σ cash + Σ in-kind (fair market value) ≤ ₹12,000

Senior cap · ₹20,000

Same scope as Junior. Bought-in bearings, axles, wheels and outsourced SLS or CNC services count at the invoiced or quoted price.

Σ cash + Σ in-kind (fair market value) ≤ ₹20,000

What counts as in-kind

Donated machine time, materials, printing or services are valued at what the team would have paid a commercial supplier, with a written quote or price-list reference in Section 6.4. School-owned equipment already on site counts at consumables cost only.

The cap is not a fundraising target. Teams that spend far less and still build a compliant, well-reasoned car are rewarded under the enterprise criterion, not penalised.

Format & scoring

Formula Celeste follows the CelesteCon two-round framework. Round 1 is asynchronous and nationwide: the dossier, drawings and CFD are judged blind by a technical review board. Round 2 is live, on campus, and physical: the car must exist, must match its drawings, and must be defended by the people who built it.

What the timed runs do and do not decide

The timed runs are part of race day, but raw speed is not in the 200 points. A car that is fast because it was lucky with a cartridge teaches nothing; a car whose team predicted its time and can explain the gap teaches everything. So the measured time does three jobs: it decides the Pole Position Trophy, it decides the Delta Award for the most accurate prediction, and it opens cross-examination, where explaining the difference between prediction and measurement is part of the Cross-Examination score.

Tie-breaks

If two constructors finish on the same combined total, the tie is broken in this order: higher Round 2 curveball score; then higher Round 1 Calculations Ledger Integrity score; then smaller prediction delta |tmeasured − tpredicted| / tmeasured; then fewer scrutineering penalty points.

Section 03 · Problem statements

The division challenges

Each division has its own challenge, regulations, fact sheet and mandatory calculations. Read your own division in full, then read the other one: Junior teams will find the Senior reasoning useful, and Senior teams will be asked in cross-examination to explain their work as simply as a Junior team would.

All numbers in the fact sheets are CelesteCon reference values published for this competition. Where your team measures or sources a better value, use it, cite it, and say in the ledger which value you replaced.

Challenge J · Grades 6–8 · Junior division

Grid Debut

Found a constructor, build its first car, and get it through scrutineering on the first attempt.

F1-JJunior RFP · Edition VI

There is one empty garage in the CelesteCon paddock this season, and it is yours. Your team has been given a racing licence, a foam block, a rulebook and a deadline. By race day you need a name, a livery, at least one sponsor, and a car that will pass scrutineering, launch cleanly and cover 20.000 metres of straight track as quickly as its design allows.

Nobody expects a rookie constructor to beat the physics. What the jury expects is that you understand it. Why is the front of your car shaped the way it is? Where does the car's weight sit, and why there? How fast will it go, and how did you work that out? What happens to your time if the car is two grams heavy, or a wheel wobbles? A Junior team that can answer those questions with numbers is doing real engineering.

The car

Your car is powered by one CelesteCon reference 8 g CO2 cartridge, which sits in a drilled chamber at the back of the car. On the start signal, a marshal's launch pod punctures the cartridge and the escaping gas pushes the car forward. A tether line runs through two guides underneath the car so it stays in its lane. The car has four wheels, a front wing and a rear wing, and it must obey the Junior Technical Regulations, TR-J.

The constructor

You are also a business. Choose a team name and identity, give every member a real role, make a budget that stays under ₹12,000, and find at least one sponsor: a local business, a supplier who gives you materials or machine time, or an organisation that pledges support. Tell each sponsor exactly what they get in return, and keep your promises.

What makes this hard

The car must be at least 55.0 g without its cartridge. Too heavy and it is slow; too light and it fails scrutineering. The cartridge itself weighs 26.0 g, sits at the back, and moves your centre of gravity a long way rearward. And the thrust line has to sit at or above the centre of gravity, or the car will try to lift its front wheels at launch.

Your constructor must

  • Name the team and assign 2–4 roles, for example team principal, aerodynamicist, design & manufacturing engineer, and enterprise manager.
  • Design one car that meets every clause of TR-J.
  • Model it as a full 3D assembly and produce the four controlled drawings.
  • Carry out a flow study comparing at least two design versions.
  • Complete calculations J-C1 to J-C10 in the ledger.
  • Plan the build, build the car, and bring it to Round 2 if shortlisted.
  • Secure one sponsor or in-kind pledge, and stay under the ₹12,000 cap.

Deliverables at a glance

  • Round 1: one PDF, ≤ 16 main-body pages, plus F1-CD-1 to F1-CD-4.
  • Round 2: the car, a ≤ 12-slide deck, a STEP file, and the pit display.

A great Junior entry

Has a simple, clean car whose every feature has a reason; a CG worked out from a real mass table; a predicted time with the working shown; honest photographs of the build, mistakes included; and a sponsor who would happily sponsor them again.

Balance and stability

  • State exactly how the test was done so another team could repeat it.
Goes in  §2.3, F1-CD-4

Decide how each part will be made, then make it.

  • Body from the official block by CNC, by 3D printing, or by careful hand-shaping to templates.

  • A mass check after each step, recorded in a table.

J.3Mandatory calculations J-C1 to J-C10

Every calculation goes in the Section 7 ledger with inputs, formula, working, result with units, and a check. The reference answers below are for the CelesteCon reference car J-REF (55.0 g, A = 2,000 mm², CD 0.70, CL 0.25, μr 0.020, wheels Ø 28 mm). Your numbers will differ; your method should not.

J-C1AERO

Projected frontal area

Drag grows with the area the air has to push past. Measure it from your CAD front view (most CAD tools can project and measure an outline) or by tracing on 1 mm graph paper.

A = area of the front-view silhouette, mm² → m² (÷ 10⁶)

Include wheels and wings. Compare with the 85 × 65 mm bounding box, 5,525 mm².

J-REF: A = 2,000 mm² = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m², 36% of the box.

J-C2MASS

Launch mass and weight

The cartridge rides with the car. At launch the car carries a full cartridge; after the gas is gone it carries an empty one.

mlaunch = mcar + 26.0 g · mcoast = mcar + 18.0 g · W = m·g

Work in kilograms for every force calculation.

J-REF: mlaunch = 81.0 g, W = 0.795 N; mcoast = 73.0 g.

J-C3LAUNCH

Peak and average acceleration

At the instant of puncture the car is still, so drag and friction are almost zero and nearly all the thrust accelerates the car. Express the answer in g as well as m/s².

apeak = Fpeak / mlaunch · aavg = J / (mmean · tA) · g-load = a / 9.81

The average uses the whole impulse spread over the 0.30 s thrust phase.

J-REF: apeak = 22.1 / 0.081 = 273 m/s² = 27.8 g; aavg = 71.4 m/s² = 7.3 g.

J-C4SPEED

Ideal and estimated peak speed

Impulse changes momentum. If there were no losses, all of J would become the car's momentum; the loss factor η accounts for drag, friction and spinning the wheels up.

videal = J / mmean · vpeak = η · videal

Also give vpeak in km/h (× 3.6). Say which value of η you used and why.

J-REF: videal = 1.65 / 0.077 = 21.43 m/s; vpeak = 18.86 m/s = 67.9 km/h.

J-C5DRAG

Aerodynamic drag at peak speed

Drag is the biggest force slowing the car after the thrust has gone. It grows with the square of speed, so it is largest at peak speed.

q = ½ ρ v² · D = q · A · CD

q is the dynamic pressure in pascals. State where your CD came from.

J-REF: q = 204.5 Pa; D = 204.5 × 0.002 × 0.70 = 0.286 N.

J-C6GRIP

Downforce and rolling resistance

Downforce pushes the car onto the track. On a straight tethered track that does not help it corner; it adds to the load on the wheels and so adds rolling resistance. Show how big that cost is.

L = q · A · CL · Frr = μr · (mcoast·g + L)

Then state what share of Frr is caused by downforce.

J-REF: L = 0.102 N; Frr = 0.020 × (0.716 + 0.102) = 0.0164 N; downforce adds 0.0020 N, under 1% of drag.

J-C7TIME

Predicted 20 m run time

Split the run into a thrust phase and a coasting phase. This two-phase model is deliberately cautious because it uses the peak-speed forces for the whole coast.

sA = 0.75 · vpeak · tA · ac = (D + Frr) / mcoast · 20 − sA = vpeaktB − ½actB² · t = tA + tB

Solve the quadratic for tB and take the smaller positive root. Give t to 3 decimal places. This is your locked prediction for the Delta Award.

J-REF: sA = 4.24 m; ac = 4.15 m/s²; tB = 0.931 s; t = 1.231 s. The Section 04 calculator gives 1.189 s for the same car.

J-C8BALANCE

Centre of gravity, with and without cartridge

The cartridge is a third of the launch mass and sits at the back. Find where the CG is before and after it goes in, and check the centre of pressure is behind it.

xcg = Σ(mi · xi) / Σmi · x measured from the nose

Estimate xcp by cutting out a card silhouette of the side view and finding its balance point. Report the gap xcp − xcg in mm.

Worked example: body 36.5 g @ 102 mm, front wing 2.0 g @ 12, rear wing 2.5 g @ 192, front wheel set 7.0 g @ 28, rear wheel set 7.0 g @ 165 → xcg = 101.4 mm; with cartridge 26.0 g @ 172.5 mm → 124.2 mm.

J-C9WHEELS

Wheel speed at peak velocity

Your wheels spin astonishingly fast. That is why wobble, poor alignment and rough bores cost so much time.

rpm = vpeak / (π · dw) × 60

dw in metres. Compare your answer with a kitchen mixer (about 1,000 rpm).

J-REF: 18.86 / (π × 0.028) × 60 = 12,864 rpm.

J-C10COMPLY

Mass compliance margin and ballast plan

Paint, glue and decals add mass you cannot model exactly, and scales differ. Plan a small safety margin above 55.0 g, and decide where ballast would go.

margin = mmeasured − 55.0 g · ballast needed = max(0, 55.3 g − mCAD)

Target 55.3 g. Place ballast so that it moves xcg forward, not back, and show the new xcg.

Example: mCAD = 53.8 g → 1.5 g ballast needed. Added at 60 mm from the nose of the J-C8 worked-example car (55.0 g, xcg 101.4 mm), 1.5 g moves the no-cartridge CG to 100.3 mm.

Challenge S · Grades 9–12 · Senior division

Race the Floor

A regulation reset year: the underbody is now the car. Engineer it, prove it in CFD, and make it survive a cold launch.

F1-SSenior RFP · Edition VI

The 2026 Senior Technical Regulations, TR-S, are a reset. Wing-dominated cars that hid behind large endplates are out. From this season, every Senior car must generate a real share of its downforce from a pair of venturi tunnels under the floor, must pass a front-wing deflection test at scrutineering, and must be quoted at two launch conditions: a warm track hall and a cold one. The teams that understood ground effect first will own the season.

Your constructor has one car to get right. It must weigh no less than 50.00 g, measured on a 0.01 g balance. It must keep a tether channel, four wheel clear-view zones, a virtual cockpit box and a launch-striker zone free of bodywork. Its underbody tunnels must narrow to a throat no larger than 85% of their inlet area. And it must still cover 20.000 m faster than its rivals, in a hall that may be ten degrees colder than the one you designed for.

The engineering problem

This is a force-budget problem with coupled constraints. Downforce stabilises the car in pitch and keeps it from binding on the tether, but it costs induced drag and rolling resistance. The floor makes downforce more efficiently than wings do, but only if its throat, diffuser and sealing are right, and only if the CFD proving it has converged. The cartridge delivers 22 N for a few hundredths of a second at 25 °C, and nearly a third less at 10 °C, because the carbon dioxide inside is a saturated liquid whose pressure falls steeply with temperature. The car sits at the minimum mass, so translational mass is fixed, but rotating mass is not. Every one of those facts has a price in milliseconds, and your dossier must show you know each price.

The constructor problem

The budget cap is ₹20,000. Ceramic hybrid bearings, titanium axles, SLS carbon-filled nylon wings and outsourced five-axis machining all cost money, and they do not all buy the same amount of time. Your enterprise portfolio must include a cost-per-millisecond trade study that ranks your spending options, and a sponsorship model that tells each sponsor what it receives and what that is worth.

Manufacturing routes

Route A is the standard route: the body is CNC-machined, three-axis or better, from the official block or a block of identical specification. Route B permits a fully additively manufactured body for teams without CNC access. The route is declared at registration and both are judged on the same rubric: what matters is how well the chosen process was engineered, controlled and justified.

Your constructor must

  • Organise as an engineering team with roles, design reviews and version-controlled CAD.
  • Design one car that meets every clause of TR-S, including FR-1, FR-2 and DT-1.
  • Run a CFD campaign with a named solver, stated boundary conditions and a three-level mesh study.
  • Model launch thermodynamics and quote the car at DP-A and DP-B.
  • Complete calculations S-C1 to S-C14 in the ledger.
  • Manufacture the car by Route A or B and bring it to Round 2 if shortlisted.
  • Stay under ₹20,000 and model sponsor return on investment.

Deliverables at a glance

  • Round 1: one PDF, ≤ 26 main-body pages, plus F1-CD-1 to F1-CD-4.
  • Round 2: the car, a ≤ 15-slide deck, a STEP file, the native CAD file for the live walkthrough, and the pit display.

S-C9STABILITY

Static margin and launch pitch check

The centre of pressure must sit behind the centre of gravity. And with thrust 30 times the car's weight, a millimetre of thrust-line offset decides whether the nose stays down.

SM = (xcp − xcg)/L ≥ 0.05 · Rf = [W₀(xr − xcg) + Fpeak(ht − hcg)] / wb > 0

Report SM with full and empty cartridge, and the front-axle load at peak thrust.

S-REF: SM = 13/200 = 0.065; Rf = (0.746 × 52 + 22.11 × 2.5)/140 = 0.672 N (static 0.277 N). With ht 2.5 mm below CG, Rf = −0.118 N: the front lifts.

S-C10INERTIA

Wheel inertia and energy fraction

At minimum mass, translational mass is fixed but rotating inertia is not. Find what share of the car's kinetic energy is locked in its wheels.

I = ½mwr² · ω = v/r · f = Σ½Iω² / (½Mv² + Σ½Iω²)

Also give rpm at peak speed and the equivalent translational mass of the wheel set.

S-REF: 14,664 rpm; I = 2.00 × 10⁻⁷ kg·m² per wheel; f = 6.1%; equivalent mass 4.4 g.

S-C11STRUCTURE

Axle shear under launch shock

Each axle must accelerate its wheels in translation and spin them up. Compute the shear, compare with the material, and then identify which part of the car is actually the critical load path.

Fx = 1.5·mw·apeak · V = √(Fx² + Fz²) · τmax = (4/3)·V/(πd²/4)

Fz = Rf/2 per front wheel. State the safety factor.

S-REF, Ti axle Ø 3.0 mm: Fx = 0.907 N, V = 0.967 N, τmax = 0.18 MPa; factor about 3,000 against 550 MPa. The axle is not the weak link.

S-C12AEROELASTIC

Wingtip deflection: aero load, DT-1 and launch inertia

Model each front-wing half as a cantilever. Compare three loads: peak aerodynamic load, the DT-1 test load, and the wing's own inertia at 28 g.

I = b·t³/12 · δUDL = wℓ⁴/(8EI) · δtip = Pℓ³/(3EI)

Inertial load acts chordwise, so use I = t·b³/12 for it. Choose the material that passes DT-1 with margin.

ℓ 32, c 14, t 1.2 mm: PA12-CF DT-1 δ = 0.59 mm (pass); Tough PLA 1.48 mm (fail). Aero δ = 0.011 mm. Inertia 0.157 N, six times the aero load, but only 0.0005 mm chordwise.

S-C13TIME

Total 20 m elapsed time prediction

Integrate the equation of motion numerically with time step Δt ≤ 1 ms, including varying mass, drag, downforce-loaded rolling resistance and wheel inertia. Quote to 3 decimal places.

(m + 2mw)·dv/dt = F(t) − ½ρv²ACD − μr(mg + ½ρv²ACL) · dm/dt = −F/vₑ

Report DP-A and DP-B times, peak speed and the sensitivity ∂t/∂m in ms per gram. The DP-A time is your locked prediction.

S-REF: DP-A 1.066 s, vpeak 20.73 m/s at 0.343 s; DP-B 1.503 s; +1 g costs 12.2 ms.

S-C14ENTERPRISE

Cost per millisecond and sponsor return

Rank at least four spending options by rupees per millisecond saved, then model what each sponsor tier receives and what it is worth to them.

₹/ms = Δcost / Δt · ROI = (Vsponsor − Csponsor) / Csponsor

Vsponsor must list its assumptions (reach, impressions, rate per thousand, non-media value) with sources.

S-REF, ballast kept at 50.00 g: wheels 2.2 → 1.6 g, −14.6 ms for ₹1,200 (₹82/ms); A 1,600 → 1,450 mm², −6.2 ms; CD 0.55 → 0.50, −6.0 ms; steel → ceramic bearings, −1.1 ms for ₹1,600 (₹1,455/ms).

4.1Why there is no fill-in template

Packaging is the design

Where the chamber sits decides the CG, which decides the ballast, which decides the static margin. A template with a box for "CG" would let a team fill it in without ever discovering that chain. The structure below tells you what must be proven, not what to write.

Aerodynamics is a trade, not a list

A floor that makes downforce efficiently also changes the pitch balance, the ride-height sensitivity and the machining strategy. Reviewers reward dossiers whose sections talk to each other, with forward and backward references, rather than isolated answers.

Manufacturing has no default

A three-axis roughing strategy, a hand-shaped Junior body and an SLS-printed Senior body are all legitimate and all judged on the same criteria. What matters is the reasoning from material to process to tolerance to test, and that cannot be forced into a blank.

    Official competition rules & integrity architecture

    These twelve rules govern Formula Celeste at CelesteCon 2026. The Technical Regulations TR-J and TR-S in Section 03 form part of these rules. Where the wording of a rule is unclear, the interpretation of the organisers under R-12 applies. Questions about the rules may be sent through the portal until seven days before the Round 1 deadline; answers that change how a rule is read are published to every team.

    R-01

    Eligibility & teams

    1. 1.1
      Open to students enrolled in Grades 6–12 at any recognised school in India for the 2026–27 academic year. Junior division: Grades 6–8. Senior division: Grades 9–12.
    2. 1.2
      A team has 2–4 students from the same school and one faculty advisor who is a teacher at that school. A team with members in both grade bands competes in the division of its most senior member.
    3. 1.3
      A school may enter at most two teams per division. A student may be in only one Formula Celeste team, but may take part in other CelesteCon competitions.
    4. 1.4
      Team membership is locked at registration. One substitution per team is permitted before the Round 1 deadline, by the advisor through the portal, for a documented reason.
    5. 1.5
      Students of the host school compete on equal terms. No member of AEROSS involved in organising, reviewing or judging Formula Celeste may compete in it.
    R-02

    Registration, blind codes & communication

    1. 2.1
      Registration is through the CelesteCon portal only. The advisor registers the team, confirms eligibility, and for Senior teams declares the manufacturing route, A or B.
    2. 2.2
      Each team receives a blind code (F1J-### or F1S-###). Reviewers in Round 1 see only the code. The mapping between codes and schools is held by one designated organiser who does not review or judge.
    3. 2.3
      All official communication goes through the portal or the registered advisor's email. Teams must not contact reviewers or jurors about the competition before results are published.
    4. 2.4
      Each registered team receives one official model block. Additional blocks may be bought from the organisers at cost; blocks of identical specification from other sources are permitted if declared in F1-CD-2.

    R-05

    Originality & AI governance

    Read in full before you start · applies to Round 1 and Round 2

    Formula Celeste tests your team's own engineering judgement. The review board and the jury must be able to trust that the design, the CAD geometry, the CFD setups, the numbers, the words and the images in your dossier and presentation are yours.

    This rule is deliberately specific so that no team is unsure what is allowed. If a use of a tool is not clearly listed as allowed below, treat it as not allowed, or ask the organisers in writing before the deadline.

    5.1 What counts as "AI"

    For this competition, AI means any software that generates, rewrites, completes, translates, summarises, designs, optimises or suggests content (text, numbers, tables, code, sketches, 3D geometry, meshes, drawings, images, audio, video or slides) instead of simply recording, displaying or calculating exactly what you typed, drew, modelled or entered. This includes AI built into everyday apps and CAD or CFD packages, whether or not the feature is labelled "AI".

    It counts as using AI whether you typed a prompt, clicked a button such as "Generate", "Rewrite", "Improve", "Magic Design", "Help me write", "Generative design", "Auto-shape" or "Optimise shape", accepted a suggested phrase, or let a CAD or solver feature create or reshape geometry for you. Not knowing that a feature uses AI is not a defence.

    5.3 The three-tier integrity model

    Tier 1
    Permitted standard tools

    Ordinary tools that record, draw, calculate or check your own work. No declaration needed beyond listing software and version in 7.4.

    • Parametric CAD: Fusion, SolidWorks, Onshape, Inventor, CATIA, Solid Edge, FreeCAD, where your team creates every sketch, feature and dimension, with generative and AI features off or unused.
    • Deterministic CFD solvers: Ansys Fluent, OpenFOAM, SimScale, Autodesk CFD, SOLIDWORKS Flow Simulation, run on geometry, meshes and settings your team defined.
    • CAM software generating toolpaths from strategies and parameters your team chose, and slicers for 3D printing.
    • Spreadsheets and code with formulas and programs your team wrote; the calculator on this page.
    • Word processors with AI features off; basic spell-check accepted one word at a time.
    • Official manufacturer CAD of bought-in bearings, axles or wheels, credited.
    • Manual web research and citation managers for sources you found.
    Tier 2
    Permitted with mandatory disclosure

    Limited research help. Every use must be recorded in the 7.4 disclosure ledger: tool, date, what you asked, what you did with the answer.

    • Conceptual background research: asking an AI chatbot to explain a concept (for example, "what is choked flow?") that you then study from a real source. You may not copy or paraphrase its answer.
    • Engineering paper discovery: using AI search to find papers, reports or datasheets, as long as you open, read and cite the original, never the AI tool.
    • Formula and software troubleshooting: asking why a spreadsheet formula your team wrote returns an error, or how a CAD, CAM or CFD setting works.
    • Checking finished working: asking whether your team's completed calculation has an error. The method, the model and the numbers must remain your own.
    Tier 3
    Strictly prohibited

    Any use in which AI produces, rewrites, designs or decides part of your submission or presentation.

    • AI generating the car or its parts: text-to-CAD, text-to-3D, image-to-3D, generative design, AI topology optimisation, AI-optimised wings, floors or bodies.
    • AI generating or altering CFD: AI surrogate predictions presented as results, AI-built meshes or setups, and any invented, "estimated" or hallucinated CFD numbers.
    • AI writing: generating, rewriting, paraphrasing, summarising or translating any dossier text, captions or slide text.
    • AI calculating: solving the mandatory calculations or writing the integrator code for you.
    • AI visuals: generated logos, liveries, renders, photographs, diagrams, or AI-generated slide decks.
    • AI in Round 2: any AI tool during the pitch, cross-examination or curveball.

    5.5 Audit and verification protocol

    1. Random dossier audit. The review board may request, within 48 hours, any team's native CAD files, CFD case files, CAM files, spreadsheets, integrator code and build photographs with original metadata.

    6.1Technical motorsport FAQ

    Q01

    Should we CNC-machine the body or 3D print it?

    Senior teams choose at registration: Route A (CNC-machined from the official block) or Route B (fully additive). Junior teams may machine, print or hand-shape. Neither route scores higher by itself.

    Machining foam gives a light body with a good surface after sealing, but thin wings and sharp internal corners are limited by tool diameter. Printing gives freedom for tunnels and thin features, but a solid print is heavy: a 200 mm body printed at 100% infill in PA12 would weigh well over the minimum, so you must design shells and internal structure, and you must think about layer direction relative to launch loads. Many strong cars combine a machined body with printed wings. What reviewers reward is the reasoning and the evidence that the process was controlled.

    Q02

    How close to the minimum mass should we build?

    Close, but never under. Junior cars are weighed to 0.1 g against 55.0 g; Senior cars to 0.01 g against 50.00 g, strictly. There is no tolerance below the minimum. Balances differ, paint absorbs moisture, and decals peel, so target about 0.3 g over (Junior 55.3 g, Senior 50.20–50.40 g). At S-REF, each extra gram costs about 12 ms, so 0.3 g of margin costs about 4 ms: cheap insurance compared with a voided run under R-07.6.

    Q03

    Can we buy wheels, axles and bearings, including imported ceramic bearings?

    Yes. Bought-in wheels, axles, bearings and bushings are allowed in both divisions, from any supplier, including imported miniature ceramic hybrid bearings. They must be listed in F1-CD-2 with supplier and price, count towards the budget cap at the invoiced price including shipping and duty, and meet TR-J or TR-S. Remember S-C14: on S-REF, ceramic bearings save about 1 ms, while cutting wheel mass from 2.2 to 1.6 g saves about 15 ms. Buy the time, not the label.

    Q04

    Which CFD software, and how fine should our mesh be?

    Any deterministic solver is allowed: Ansys (including student licences), OpenFOAM, SimScale, Autodesk CFD, SOLIDWORKS Flow Simulation and others. Junior teams do not need CFD; a well-run smoke or tuft test scores just as well.

    For Senior teams there is no required cell count. Required is evidence that your answer no longer depends on the mesh: run three meshes, each refined by a roughly constant ratio (for example 1.5× in each direction), and show CD and CL changing by less than 2% between the two finest. Refine where it matters: wing leading and trailing edges, the tunnel throat, the diffuser and the wheel contact regions. Use a moving ground at car speed, state whether wheels rotate, give your turbulence model and first-cell height or y⁺, and show residual and force-monitor histories. A domain of about 3 car lengths ahead, 8 behind and 3 to each side and above is a reasonable start; test it.

    Q05

    How do we stop the car binding on the tether line?

    Binding happens when the guides are not in line with the tether, when a guide hole is too tight or rough, or when the car pitches or yaws under launch so the guides press on the line. Put both guides exactly on the car's centreline, with holes at the larger end of the allowed range and smooth, rounded edges. Keep the thrust line at or above the CG (S-C9) so the nose stays down, and keep the CP behind the CG so the car points straight. Test by sliding the car along a taut line at home: it should run the length without catching when tilted slightly.

    Q06

    Can we put a real company's logo on our car?

    Only a sponsor's logo, and only with the sponsor's written permission, which you keep and may be asked to show. You may not use the name, logo or livery of a real Formula 1 team, car manufacturer or F1 sponsor that has not sponsored you, and you may not use the Formula 1, F1, FIA or F1 in Schools marks. Tobacco, alcohol, gambling and weapons brands are never permitted. In the Round 1 PDF, a sponsor's name is fine, but it must not reveal your school or city.

    Q07

    How do we bring the car to DPS R.K. Puram?

    By hand, in a rigid box. A 50 g car with 1 mm wings does not survive a courier. Make a foam cut-out that holds the car by the body only, with the wings and wheels free of contact, and pack spares (wings, wheels, axles, guides) in the same box. Flying teams should carry the box as cabin baggage. Do not bring CO₂ cartridges: they are not allowed on campus and are restricted on aircraft. Bring a copy of F1-CD-1 and F1-CD-3, your rectification kit and a small balance if you have one.

    Q08

    Does the car have to be finished before Round 1?

    No, but you need real manufacturing evidence. Round 1 must show your manufacturing plan and at least one manufactured prototype or test part (a machined or printed body, a wing test piece, a wheel set on a test rig) with photographs and measurements. A complete car is required only for Round 2. Teams that have built and tested a car before the deadline naturally have more to show in Section 5.0 and more evidence for their predictions.

    Q09

    Can we change the car after Round 1?

    Yes. Improve it as much as you like, but declare every change in a one-page change log uploaded with your Round 2 deck (R-06.4), and update your drawings for the scrutineers. Your locked prediction does not change, and the jury will ask what each change did to your time. A car that differs materially from the dossier without a declared change is penalised under R-07.

    Q10

    What if our measured time is far from our prediction?

    That is normal, and it is not directly penalised. Real cars lose time to tether friction, wheel wobble, surface roughness and cartridge variation that no simple model captures. What earns marks in cross-examination is explaining the gap: which assumptions were optimistic, how many milliseconds each probably cost, and how you would test that. A team that predicted 1.066 s, ran 1.142 s and can account for most of the 76 ms is doing exactly what race engineers do.

    Q11

    Why does temperature matter so much for the cartridge?

    The cartridge contains liquid carbon dioxide at its saturation pressure, which depends strongly on temperature: about 64 bar at 25 °C but 45 bar at 10 °C. The escaping flow is choked at the puncture, and choked-flow thrust is proportional to the pressure behind it, so a cold cartridge gives roughly 30% less thrust. That is why Senior teams quote DP-B, and why marshals store race-day cartridges at hall temperature. Try it in the calculator in Section 04.

    Q12

    Can we test launch our car at school?

    Yes, if your school allows it and an adult supervises every launch. Use a purpose-made launch system, safety glasses for everyone nearby, a tethered line and a soft catch at the end. Never hold a cartridge while it is punctured, never modify or heat a cartridge, and store cartridges away from heat. If you cannot test launches safely, ramp or coast-down tests on the tether line still give useful friction and drag data.

    Q13

    Can a Junior team go further and use Senior methods?

    Yes. Junior teams may run CFD, add underbody tunnels or complete Senior calculations, and reviewers will credit correct work. You are still judged against TR-J and the Junior rubric, and the ten J-C calculations remain mandatory. Do the Junior requirements well first.

    Q14

    Can we use an AI chatbot to learn the physics?

    You may ask an AI tool to explain a concept, then learn it properly from a real source that you cite, and you must log every such use in Section 7.4 (Tier 2). You may not let AI write, calculate, design, mesh or draw anything that goes into your dossier, model or slides (Tier 3). The easiest rule of thumb: if the AI's output could be pasted into your submission, you are not allowed to use it that way.

    Q15

    Is Formula Celeste part of F1 in Schools?

    No. Formula Celeste is an independent CelesteCon competition whose format is inspired by F1 in Schools and Formula Student, and whose regulations draw on the engineering of Formula 1. It is not organised, sponsored or endorsed by Formula 1, the FIA or F1 in Schools, unless and until CelesteCon formally announces a partnership. Our regulations are our own, so do not assume a rule from another competition applies here.
