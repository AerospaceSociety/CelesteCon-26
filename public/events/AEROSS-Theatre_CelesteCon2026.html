AEROSSTheatreThe Proscenium & Spotlight Challenge · Performing arts of CelesteCon

Earn the stage with what you know. Hold it with what you wrote. Keep it when the lights change without warning.

AEROSS Theatre is the open-stage performing arts competition of CelesteCon 2026, open to school delegations across India in Grades 6–12. Stand-up, the one-act play, physical theatre, musical satire, expressive movement and mixed media all compete on the same boards and under the same standard: original work, sharp wit, disciplined stagecraft and complete command of the room, without a single cheap shot.

In Round 1, every registered performer sits an individual, optically proctored screening paper on Quizzitch, AEROSS's own examination platform, and the scores are pooled into one composite team index. Finalist delegations are then called to DPS R.K. Puram, New Delhi, for Round 2: a live stage showcase of 7:30 to 10:00 minutes, followed at once by the Spotlight Curveball, a sealed impromptu twist with sixty seconds to huddle and two minutes to answer it on stage, and a five-minute Artistic Intent defence before the jury.

Section 01 · The brief & creative freedom

The stage is an argument with a body

Every CelesteCon competition asks students to build something that has to survive contact with reality: a settlement that must keep people alive, an aircraft that must fly, an argument that must hold under cross-examination. AEROSS Theatre asks the same of a performance. A script is a hypothesis about an audience. The house tests it live, in real time, and it answers in laughter, silence, attention or restlessness that no amount of rehearsal can fake.

The brief is therefore deliberately open. Troupes choose their form, their tone and their story. What they cannot choose is the standard: original writing, disciplined stagecraft, ideas worth an audience's ten minutes, and a performance that stays sharp and dignified from the first cue to the last word of the defence.

Satire is the oldest form of peer review. It asks a society to look at itself, and it only works when the audience trusts the person holding the mirror.

Theatre as intellectual combat

A strong performance makes a claim about the world and defends it with every tool the stage allows: character, structure, rhythm, silence, light and the body. A stand-up set that exposes the logic of a corporate launch press release is making an argument. So is a two-hander about an astronaut who can no longer remember the colour of rain. The jury reads both the same way: what does this piece believe, and how well does it make the house believe it too?

Catharsis and the sharp edge

Aristotle described tragedy as a purging of pity and fear; the Natyashastra describes rasa, the distilled emotional flavour an audience savours. Comedy and satire have their own catharsis: the release of seeing a shared absurdity named precisely. AEROSS Theatre rewards both. The edge that matters is insight. A line that makes the house laugh and then think about why it laughed is worth more than any line that only shocks.

Autonomy with discipline

Creative freedom here means freedom of form, subject, tone, structure and language. It does not mean freedom from craft or from decorum. The troupe writes its own piece, designs its own cue sheet, blocks its own stage and answers for every choice in the defence. The boundaries in R-05 are not a limit on wit; they are the conditions under which a school auditorium full of students, teachers and parents can trust the performer enough to laugh.

What the jury will ask

  • Is this piece yours, in every line and every beat?
  • Does it earn its running time, or does it fill it?
  • Does it have something to say about people, power, science, or the future?
  • Does the troupe own the space, the microphone and the silence?
  • When the lights change without warning, who is still in control?

The four non-negotiable tests

I

The originality & script integrity test

The writing is the troupe's own: a fresh perspective, fresh jokes, fresh dramatic situations.

  • No sketches, bits or routines lifted from the internet, specials, television or stage.
  • No stock premises delivered as if new; a familiar form must carry a new angle.
  • No AI-written scripts, jokes or lyrics, in whole or in part.
  • Adaptations of public-domain works are declared and transformed, not recited.

Fails when: a juror recognises the material, the defence cannot explain how it was written, or the submitted script and the live piece do not match.

II

The decorum & taste test

Wit that cuts through insight, never through cheap shock. Zero profanity, zero vulgarity, zero slurs, zero targeting of any faith, community, identity or real individual.

  • Satire aims at ideas, systems, institutions, habits and archetypes.
  • Discomfort is allowed when it serves the piece; degradation is not.
  • The standard applies to costume, gesture, props, music, projections and ad-libs alike.

Fails when: any breach listed in R-05.4 occurs on stage. The result is immediate stage disqualification, not a deduction.

III

The stage command & theatrical economy test

Use of space, vocal projection, comic timing, emotional truth and spatial blocking. Every minute, cue and prop is doing work.

  • Blocking that reads from every seat, including the sightline-shadowed corners.
  • Transitions that are fast, clean and part of the storytelling.
  • A running time inside the 7:30–10:00 window without rushing the ending.

Fails when: long blackouts, inaudible lines, dead air, or props that take longer to set than to use.

IV

The impromptu composure test

Grace, agility and wit under the live Spotlight Curveball: sixty seconds to huddle, two minutes to answer on stage, and no second take.

  • The response uses the troupe's own world and characters, transformed by the prompt.
  • The whole ensemble contributes; nobody freezes and nobody is abandoned.
  • Decorum holds under pressure exactly as it did in the scripted piece.

Fails when: the troupe ignores the prompt, performs an unrelated prepared bit, or runs out of ideas before the minute mark.

01.1Freedom and its frame

What this stage is not

  • A recitation of a poem or a speech learnt by heart and delivered to the back wall.
  • A re-enactment of a viral sketch, a televised play or a comedy special.
  • Slapstick for its own sake, or a sequence of gags with no idea underneath.
  • Shock humour: crude jokes, insults, innuendo or put-downs aimed at people.
  • A performance that leans on recorded vocals, a scrolling script or a projection doing the acting.
  • An imitation of a teacher, a classmate, a juror or any identifiable real person.

What this stage is

  • An original piece in any form: stand-up, play, physical theatre, musical satire, movement or mixed media.
  • A point of view about the world, argued with craft, structure and timing.
  • Sophisticated, satirical and culturally engaging, with room for the serious and the absurd alike.
  • Comedy that earns its laughs through observation, character, escalation and surprise.
  • Live, present and ensemble-driven: every performer matters to the piece.
  • A performance that a principal, a parent and a student can enjoy in the same row.

01.2Forms welcome on the boards

Form A

Stand-up & sketch

Solo or ensemble observational comedy, character sets, double acts and linked sketches. Rewarded for premise, structure, callbacks and audience command rather than punchline density.

Form B

The one-act play

A complete dramatic arc in under ten minutes: comedy, drama, tragicomedy, absurdist or verse. Rewarded for economy of exposition, dialogue that carries subtext, and an ending that lands.

Form C

Physical theatre & mime

Clowning, mime, object theatre, commedia-style masks and devised movement. Rewarded for precision, control, spatial storytelling and the clarity of a story told largely without words.

Form D

Musical satire

Original songs, parody lyrics on declared melodies, patter songs and live instrumentation. Rewarded for lyrical wit, musicality and the argument the song makes, not for vocal display alone.

Form E

Expressive movement & dance

Contemporary, classical, folk-fusion or devised choreography that carries a narrative or a thematic idea. Rewarded for storytelling through the body and a clear relationship to the chosen track.

Form F

Mixed media & hybrid

Any combination of the above, with projection, soundscape or live foley in support. Rewarded when every medium is doing work the others cannot, and the live performers stay at the centre.

Forms are declared on the Repertoire Declaration so the jury and the technical crew can prepare, but they are not separate categories. Every finalist is judged on the same 100-point rubric and competes for the same podium.

01.3The pillars of adjudication

01

Dramaturgy & writing

Rubric · 25 points

Originality of premise, structure and arc, economy of scene, quality of dialogue or material, and a coherent ending. Finalised by the jury after the Artistic Intent defence.

Asks: is every line yours, and does every line earn its place?

02

Physical & vocal execution

Rubric · 25 points

Projection and diction, timing and pace, physical control, use of the nine zones, microphone discipline, and the synergy of the ensemble as a single organism.

Asks: can the last row hear, see and feel every beat?

03

Satirical & thematic depth

Rubric · 20 points

Relevance to the chosen track, the intelligence of the satire or the depth of the drama, layers of meaning, and the precision with which the piece hits its real target.

Asks: what does this piece know that the audience did not?

04

Spontaneity

Rubric · 20 points

The Spotlight Curveball response: speed of adaptation, inventiveness, ensemble listening, commitment and decorum under pressure, judged live and only once.

Asks: who is still in command when the script is gone?

A fifth criterion, Time Discipline & Technical Decorum (10 points), is scored by the stage manager's timing record and the jury's technical observation. The full rubric, with band descriptors, is in Section 06.

02.1The scale rule · who reaches the stage

≤ 15

Direct mode · every eligible delegation performs

If 15 or fewer delegations complete registration nationwide, every delegation that is eligible after the Quizzitch integrity audit is called to DPS R.K. Puram. All of them compete on the live stage directly for the top three places and the signature awards.

Quizzitch still runs and is still compulsory. In direct mode it decides the seed order, which sets the order in which delegations choose their performance slot, and it awards the Quizzitch Screening Laureate.

16+ → 10

Qualifying mode · the top 10 advance

If 16 or more delegations register, the Quizzitch Composite Team Index ranks them nationally and the top 10 are called to the finale, however many delegations have entered. The ten finalists compete on the live stage for the top three places and the signature awards.

If a finalist withdraws before repertoire submission closes, the next-ranked delegation is invited. After that point, no replacement is made and the running order closes up.

The mode is fixed on the count of delegations whose registration is complete when registration closes, and it is published to every advisor before the Quizzitch window opens, so every troupe knows whether the paper is a qualifying gate or a seeding paper before sitting it. Eligibility means at least two members of the delegation sat the paper and no Level 3 integrity finding stands against the delegation (R-04).

Seeds choose, in order

After the shortlist, delegations choose their performance slot from the published run-sheet in seed order: seed 1 chooses first. Slots are grouped into sessions of up to five performances, with a ten-minute interval between sessions. A delegation that does not choose within the deadline is allocated the earliest open slot.

The stage manager's clock starts on the first deliberate light cue, sound cue, word or movement after the house goes to black, and stops on the final blackout, bow or the last word, whichever comes first. Section 03 · The Quizzitch prelims & screening engine

Know the world before you satirise it

Satire depends on knowing precisely what is being satirised; drama depends on understanding the people and ideas it portrays. The Quizzitch screening checks that every performer in a delegation brings that grounding to the stage: in the history and devices of theatre, in rhetoric, in the science and exploration that CelesteCon celebrates, in the paradoxes that make good drama, and in the media and politics that good satire interrogates.

The paper is sat individually, under the same optically proctored conditions used across CelesteCon 2026, and the scores are pooled so that the whole troupe, not one well-read member, carries the delegation through the gate.

03.1Why performers sit a paper

Grounding

Literacy before license

A troupe that knows how Aristophanes mocked Athens, how Brecht broke the fourth wall and why the Natyashastra built drama around rasa writes better satire than one that only knows last week's trends. The paper rewards the reading behind the writing.

Reasoning

Wit is a form of logic

A punchline is an inference the audience completes. Items on paradox, fallacy and media framing test the same muscle that makes a comic premise escalate cleanly and a dramatic argument hold together.

Fairness

An objective gate at scale

When many delegations enter, a standardised, proctored paper decides the finalists on equal terms for every school in India, without judging audition videos made with unequal equipment.

03.3Screening syllabus · six modules

Q-A4 items

Classical & modern dramaturgy

Greek tragedy and Old Comedy, Aristotle's Poetics (hamartia, peripeteia, anagnorisis, catharsis), Bharata's Natyashastra and rasa theory, Sanskrit drama, Shakespeare, commedia dell'arte, Molière, Stanislavski, Brecht's alienation effect, Beckett and the Theatre of the Absurd, and modern Indian theatre from Habib Tanvir, Vijay Tendulkar, Girish Karnad and Badal Sircar.

Read: the structure of a one-act play; the five-act model; the difference between plot and story.

Q-B4 items

Rhetoric & satirical devices

Ethos, pathos and logos; verbal, situational and dramatic irony; hyperbole, litotes, bathos, zeugma and antithesis; parody, pastiche and burlesque; Horatian, Juvenalian and Menippean satire; the rule of three, callbacks, escalation and the comic reversal.

Read: Swift, Wilde, Twain, Harishankar Parsai and Shrilal Shukla, and why their targets were systems rather than people.

Q-C3 items

Science & space history

Galileo and the telescope, Sputnik and Vostok, Apollo 11 and Apollo 13, Aryabhata, Rakesh Sharma and Soyuz T-11, Chandrayaan-3, Mangalyaan, the International Space Station, and science on stage from Brecht's Life of Galileo to Michael Frayn's Copenhagen.

Read: the human stories behind landmark missions, including the failures that changed them.

Q-D3 items

Philosophical paradoxes

The Ship of Theseus, Zeno's paradoxes, the sorites (heap) paradox, the liar paradox, the Chinese Room, the Fermi paradox, the trolley problem and Buridan's ass, and how each has been dramatised or used as a comic engine.

Read: identity, memory and machine minds, the raw material of track T-II.

Q-E3 items

Global geopolitics & media analysis

The Outer Space Treaty and the idea of space as a shared domain, the overview effect, propaganda techniques, framing and agenda-setting, headline analysis, misinformation and verification, and the difference between satire, parody news and fabrication.

Read: how the same event is framed by different outlets, the raw material of track T-III.

Q-F3 items

Critical reasoning

Syllogisms and conditional logic, informal fallacies (straw man, false dilemma, ad hominem, slippery slope), correlation versus causation, data interpretation from a short table or chart, and quick estimation.

Read: spotting the flaw in a confident argument, the same skill as spotting the joke in it.

Why a mean, and why everyone counts

Delegations range from two to five performers, so a sum would reward size. The mean puts a duo and a five-person company on equal terms, and counting every registered performer means the whole troupe prepares. Worked example: a four-member delegation scoring 72, 64, 58 and 0 (one member absent) has CTI = 194 ÷ 4 = 48.50. Had the absent member sat and scored 50, the CTI would have been 61.00.

Ties in the CTI are broken in order by: (1) the higher mean combined score on modules Q-A and Q-B, the two closest to the stage; (2) fewer Level 1 advisories across the delegation's integrity ledgers; (3) the earlier mean seal time of the delegation's papers.

03.5Optical proctoring architecture

All detection runs on the candidate's own device in the browser. The video feed is not streamed or stored; the system records events, timestamps and the baseline photo to a sealed integrity ledger, which the organisers audit before any result is published.

Sensor 01

Facial presence lock

The candidate's face is compared continuously with the baseline photo captured at calibration. Leaving the frame or a change of person is flagged.

Logs: absence start and end times, presence mismatch.

Sensor 02

Multi-person detection

A second person entering the frame, fully or partly, is detected by the on-device vision model and recorded with a timestamp.

Logs: person count above one, duration.

Sensor 03

Device suppression

Phones, laptops, monitors and books in frame are detected by the COCO-SSD object model and flagged. Only blank paper and a pen are permitted on the desk.

Logs: object class, confidence, duration.

Sensor 04

Fullscreen enforcement

The paper runs only in fullscreen. Exiting fullscreen pauses the view, not the clock, until the candidate returns.

Logs: each exit, time out of fullscreen.

Sensor 05

Focus & tab discipline

Switching tabs or windows, opening other applications or losing window focus is detected by the browser and recorded.

Logs: focus-loss events, duration, tab switches.

Sensor 06

Sealed response ledger

Responses are sealed with salted client-side hashing, so no answer can be altered after submission, and every event above is written to the ledger in order.

Logs: seal hash, submit time, connection gaps.

Level 1 · Advisory
  • Brief absence from frame under 5 seconds
  • A single focus loss under 3 seconds
  • Low-confidence object flag not repeated

Recorded; no score change. Used only as a CTI tie-break.

Level 2 · Review
  • Repeated focus losses or fullscreen exits
  • Absence from frame over 5 seconds
  • A second person or a device in frame briefly

Human review of the ledger. Deduction of 5–20 marks from that candidate, or no action.

  • Impersonation or a presence mismatch confirmed
  • Assistance from another person or device
  • Recording or sharing paper content

Candidate score voided; delegation disqualified from AEROSS Theatre.

Q-A · MCQ

In Aristotle's Poetics, the moment in which a character passes from ignorance to knowledge, such as Oedipus discovering who he is, is called:

  1. Peripeteia
  2. Anagnorisis
  3. Hamartia
  4. Catharsis

B · Anagnorisis. Peripeteia is the reversal of fortune, hamartia the tragic error and catharsis the purging of pity and fear in the audience. In Oedipus Rex, the recognition and the reversal arrive together, which Aristotle considered the finest construction.

Q-B · MCQ

Jonathan Swift's A Modest Proposal (1729) recommends, in a calm and reasonable voice, a monstrous solution to poverty in Ireland. Its method is best described as:

  1. Horatian satire through gentle mockery
  2. Juvenalian satire through deadpan irony
  3. Pastiche of a contemporary pamphlet
  4. Bathos through deliberate anticlimax

B · Juvenalian satire through deadpan irony. The essay's cold, bureaucratic tone attacks the real target, the indifference of those in power, with bitter rather than gentle intent. The gap between the calm voice and the horror of the proposal is the whole engine of the piece, and a lesson in what satire aims at: the system, not the victims.

Q-C · MCQ

In April 1984, asked by the Prime Minister how India looked from space, which Indian cosmonaut replied "Saare Jahan Se Achha"?

  1. Ravish Malhotra
  2. Rakesh Sharma
  3. Shubhanshu Shukla
  4. Kalpana Chawla

B · Rakesh Sharma, aboard Salyut 7 after launching on Soyuz T-11. Ravish Malhotra was his backup. Shubhanshu Shukla flew to the International Space Station in 2025, and Kalpana Chawla flew on the Space Shuttle. One line of dialogue, delivered from orbit, became part of the national memory: a useful reminder of what a single well-chosen line can do.

Q-D · MCQ

Over twenty years every component of a space station is replaced, one module at a time. A crew member argues it is no longer the same station. This dispute is a version of:

  1. Zeno's paradox of Achilles and the tortoise
  2. The sorites paradox
  3. The Ship of Theseus
  4. Newcomb's problem

C · The Ship of Theseus. It asks whether identity survives the gradual replacement of every part. The sorites paradox concerns vague boundaries (when does a heap stop being a heap?), which is related but distinct. Both are rich premises for track T-II.

Q-E · Source

A headline reads: "Students who attend live theatre score higher in exams, study finds." The study compared exam scores of students who did and did not attend performances. Which conclusion does the evidence support?

  1. Attending theatre causes higher exam scores
  2. Theatre attendance and exam scores are associated, but other factors may explain both
  3. Students who score higher choose to attend theatre as a reward
  4. The study proves theatre should replace revision

B. An observational comparison shows association, not causation. Family background, school resources or time available could drive both. Option A is the headline's framing; option C is an alternative causal story the study equally cannot prove.

Q-F · Numerical

A troupe's 9:00 performance contains 9 scene changes, each a 12-second blackout. What percentage of the running time is spent in darkness? (Enter a number; tolerance ±0.5.)

20. 9 × 12 s = 108 s of darkness; 108 ÷ 540 s = 0.20, so 20%. One fifth of the performance in the dark is exactly the kind of lost stage time the cue sandbox in Section 04 is designed to catch before the jury does.

04

Section 04 · Thematic canvas, repertoire & stage specifications

Five doors onto one stage

Every delegation declares one of five thematic tracks. The tracks are anchors, not cages: they give the jury a lens for Thematic Resonance and give the troupe a starting provocation, while leaving form, tone, setting and story entirely to the writers. The stage itself is fixed and shared, so this section also sets out exactly what every troupe will find on the boards, and gives you a sandbox to plan cues against the curtain call and a drill to rehearse the Curveball.

04.1The five performance tracks

T-ISatire · habitat & enterprise

The Absurdity of the Horizon

Humanity finally leaves the planet and immediately brings the queue, the form in triplicate, the loyalty programme and the group chat with it.

Provocations

  • A sealed Mars habitat where the only argument that never ends is whose turn it is to clean the air filter.
  • The first commercial passenger flight to orbit, and its safety briefing, terms and conditions and seat upgrade policy.
  • A residents' welfare association meeting on a lunar base, with oxygen on the agenda under "any other business".

Forms that suit

Stand-up, sketch sequences, office farce, mock-documentary, musical satire and the corporate presentation that slowly goes wrong.

The jury looks for

Precise observation of real human habits, relocated so that we see them freshly. The best pieces make the audience laugh at a habit they recognise in themselves.

Pitfall: a string of space puns. The setting is the lens, not the joke.

T-IIDrama · mind & machine

The Ghost in the Monolith

At the edge of the known universe, a person, a machine and a memory try to decide which of them is still real.

Provocations

  • A deep-space probe's onboard intelligence, forty years out, begins writing letters to the engineers who built it, most of whom have retired.
  • The last crew member of a long-haul mission rehearses a conversation with a family who will be old when she lands.
  • A station whose every part has been replaced asks whether it is still the ship that left.

Forms that suit

The one-act play, two-hander drama, physical theatre, movement pieces with soundscape, and tragicomedy with a quiet ending.

The jury looks for

Emotional truth and restraint, a clear dramatic question, and an ending that trusts the audience. Silence and stillness are tools here, not dead air.

Pitfall: a lecture about artificial intelligence. Dramatise a person, not a position.

T-IIIPerspective · orbit & society

Earth from 400 Kilometres

From the altitude of a space station the borders disappear. Down on the ground, the arguments about them do not.

Provocations

  • An astronaut tries to explain a family dispute over a property line to a crewmate while watching the entire subcontinent pass in a few minutes.
  • Ground control, a news channel and a weather forecaster describe the same storm in three entirely different stories.
  • An alien anthropologist files a field report on how humans decide who is "from here".

Forms that suit

Satirical news formats, verbatim-style montage built from original writing, chorus work, parallel scenes and perspective-switching ensemble pieces.

The jury looks for

A genuine shift of perspective that lets the audience see a familiar social habit as strange, argued through character rather than slogans.

Pitfall: partisan commentary or named public figures. Satirise the idea, the habit and the system; never a party, a leader, a faith or a community (R-05.4).

T-IVFarce · clowning & breakdown

The Comedy of Errors in High Vacuum

Everything that can go wrong in orbit does, and the crew's pride goes wrong fastest of all.

Provocations

  • Two engineers, one airlock, one instruction manual, and no shared language except mime.
  • A spacewalk choreographed as a ballet in which the tether keeps winning.
  • The mission's most important experiment is a sandwich, and it has escaped.

Forms that suit

Clowning, mime, slapstick with precision, object theatre, farce with mistaken identity, and physical comedy set to live or recorded music.

The jury looks for

Control. The best physical comedy looks chaotic and is rehearsed to the centimetre; the escalation is logical and the characters stubborn for a reason.

Pitfall: falls and collisions with real risk. All physical comedy is choreographed and safe (R-09); nothing is thrown into the house.

T-VOpen repertoire · open canvas

Open Repertoire

Complete artistic autonomy: an original narrative of the troupe's own conception, in any form, on any subject, bound only by decorum and safety.

What to declare

A statement of intent of up to 150 words in the Repertoire Declaration: what the piece is about, what it wants the audience to feel or think, and why this form.

How it is judged

Thematic Resonance is scored against the troupe's own declared intent. The jury asks whether the piece achieved what it set out to achieve, and whether that ambition was worth an audience's time.

The jury looks for

A distinctive voice and a reason to exist. Open does not mean easier: without a track to lean on, the idea has to be strong enough to stand alone.

Pitfall: an unfocused variety show. Even an open piece needs one spine.

04.1aCreative freedom guidelines

Setting

Space is optional

Tracks T-I to T-IV take their images from spaceflight, but a piece does not have to be set in space. A T-III piece set entirely in a Delhi colony can succeed if the perspective shift is there. The connection to the declared track must be clear to the jury by the end of the performance.

Tone

Comic, serious, or both

No track demands comedy or drama. Tragicomedy is welcome everywhere. The rubric rewards the right tone for the idea, executed well. A moving T-I piece or a very funny T-II piece is entirely possible.

Language

English, Hindi or both

Performances may be in English, Hindi or a mix of the two. Brief passages in other Indian languages are welcome where the meaning is clear from context. The defence may be answered in either language.

Changing track

Declared once, refined later

The track is declared at registration. It may be changed once, up to the close of repertoire submission, by the faculty advisor through the portal. The title and synopsis may be refined freely until that point.

The ensemble

Everyone performs

Every registered performer appears on stage in the performance and in the Curveball. Roles may be unequal in size, but no registered member is a passenger. The faculty advisor does not perform or operate cues.

The audience

Interaction, with consent

Addressing the house is welcome. Bringing audience members on stage, touching them, or planting non-registered people in the house is not permitted. Any call-and-response is optional for the audience.

04.2aThe four lighting states

GW · STATE 1

General Wash

Even, warm-white light across all nine zones. The default for ensemble scenes, stand-up and the Curveball. Every face readable from every seat.

SP · STATE 2

Spotlight

A single tight pool focused on one declared zone, the rest of the stage dark. For monologue, reveal, punchline isolation and the moment that must be seen alone.

WI · STATE 3

Warm Intimate

Low amber light concentrated centre and downstage, falling off to the wings. For memory, confession, night scenes and the quiet two-hander.

BO · STATE 4

High-Contrast Blackout

Full stage black, with only safety lighting in the wings. For scene changes, endings and the sharp cut. Counts against running time like any other second.

      R-05

      Originality, decorum & script integrity

      Read in full before registering · applies from registration to the announcement of results

      AEROSS Theatre gives troupes complete freedom of form and subject. In return it asks for two things without exception: that every word, beat and idea on stage belongs to the people performing it, and that the performance holds the dignity of a school stage from the first cue to the last word of the defence.

      This rule is deliberately specific so that no delegation is unsure what is allowed. If a use of a tool or a source is not clearly listed as permitted, treat it as not permitted, or ask the organisers in writing before repertoire submission closes.

      5.1 Scope

      R-05 applies to every performer, reserve and advisor, from registration until results are final. It covers the Quizzitch paper, the Repertoire Declaration, the submitted script, cue sheet and media, the live performance, the Curveball response, the Artistic Intent defence, and conduct anywhere on campus.

      5.2 What counts as generative AI

      Generative AI means any software that generates, rewrites, paraphrases, translates, summarises or completes content (text, jokes, lyrics, dialogue, music, voice, images or video) rather than simply recording, displaying or editing exactly what you created. It includes AI built into search engines, browsers, phones, word processors, music apps and study tools, whether or not the feature is labelled "AI": chat assistants, AI writing and rewriting features, paraphrasers, AI music and voice generators, AI video and image generators, and AI joke, script or speech generators.

      5.3 The three-tier integrity model

      Tier 1
      Permitted tools

      Use freely · no declaration needed

      • Original scripts, jokes, lyrics and choreography written or devised by delegation members.
      • Standard word processors, notebooks and shared documents for writing and editing your own text.
      • Spelling and grammar correction that does not rewrite sentences.
      • Rehearsal recordings on a phone or camera for self-review.
      • Permitted backing audio: original music by delegation members, or instrumental tracks you are licensed to use.
      • Public-domain musical works, performed live or in recordings you are licensed to use, credited on the cue sheet.
      • Feedback from teachers, peers and family on a draft, provided every change is written by the troupe.
      Tier 2
      Permitted with mandatory disclosure

      Declared on the cue sheet & credits list

      • Adapting public-domain literature or theatre (for example Shakespeare, Kalidasa, Molière, Chekhov, Tagore) with attribution and genuine transformation.
      • Open-licence or royalty-free sound effects and ambiences, credited with source and licence.
      • Basic audio editing software to cut, fade, loop and level your own or licensed audio.
      • Parody lyrics written by the troupe to an existing melody, with the melody named and credited.
      • Brief quotations of published works within an original piece, attributed, totalling no more than 30 seconds of stage time.
      • Adapting your own school's previous original production, with the original authors named.
      Tier 3
      Strictly prohibited

      Any use is a Level 3 breach

      • AI-generated or AI-rewritten scripts, stand-up routines, jokes, lyrics, dialogue or scenarios, in whole or in part, including "ideas" pasted in and edited.
      • Copying or re-enacting existing stand-up specials, online sketches, televised or staged plays, reels or viral routines, with or without attribution.
      • Profanity, vulgarity, slurs or any content listed in 5.4, in any language.
      • Real-time electronic prompters during the performance: earpieces, hidden screens, phones, smart watches, or projected script text.
      • Pre-recorded lead vocals or lip-syncing to a recorded voice.
      • AI-generated voices, music, images or video in any submitted media file.

      5.4 The decorum & content code · zero tolerance

      Absolutely prohibited on stage, in any form, in any language
      • Abusive language, profanity or expletives, including bleeped, mouthed, disguised or implied versions.
      • Vulgarity, obscenity, sexual innuendo or suggestive gesture or costume.
      • Hate speech, slurs or stereotypes targeting religion, caste, community, race, ethnicity, region, language, nationality, gender, sexuality, disability or appearance.
      • Religious or communal targeting, mockery of any faith, deity, scripture, ritual or place of worship.
      • Personal defamation, impersonation or ridicule of any identifiable real individual, living or dead, public or private, including teachers, students, jurors and organisers.
      • Partisan political campaigning or content attacking a named political party or leader.
      • Depiction or glorification of self-harm, substance use, violence or cruelty for effect.
      • Body-shaming, and make-up or costume that caricatures any race, ethnicity, caste, region or community.
      • Inappropriate conduct on stage, in the wings, in the green room or in the house.

      Any breach during a performance, Curveball or defence = immediate stage disqualification.

      On a breach, the stage manager, on the instruction of the jury chair, calls a blackout and cuts sound at once. The delegation leaves the stage, receives no score, and is ineligible for every award. There is no warning, no deduction in lieu and no second performance. The decision is recorded and communicated to the advisor in writing the same day.

      5.5 Satire, real people & institutions

      Satire in AEROSS Theatre aims at ideas, systems, institutions, professions, habits and archetypes. A piece may satirise bureaucracy, corporate spaceflight, media framing, examination culture or the idea of borders. It may not portray, name or imitate an identifiable real person, and it may not target a specific real organisation in a way that would read as a factual allegation. Fictional characters and fictional companies are the tools of fair satire; use them.

      5.6 The Script Committee

      Every finalist's submitted script, lyrics and media are reviewed by a Script Committee of the organisers for decorum and safety, not for artistic merit. The Committee returns written notes within three days of submission. Changes it requires must be made before the showcase; a delegation that performs material the Committee required to be removed is treated as in breach of 5.4. A script approved by the Committee does not protect material added on stage that breaches 5.4.

      5.7 Ad-libs & the Curveball

      Natural improvisation within the submitted script, and the entire Curveball response, are unscripted by design. They are held to exactly the same code as scripted material. Pressure is not a defence: the composure test in Section 01 includes decorum under pressure.

      5.8 How originality is verified

      • Comparison. The live performance is compared with the submitted script; material departures beyond reasonable improvisation are noted by the jury.
      • Recognition. Jurors and the Script Committee flag material they recognise; the organisers may check submitted scripts against published and online sources.
      • The defence. Jurors may ask how any line, scene or song was written, by whom and in what order. A troupe that cannot explain its own writing will be referred for review.
      • Drafts. The organisers may ask the advisor for dated drafts or rehearsal notes; keep them until results are final.

      5.9 Sanctions & the integrity adjustment

      Level 1 · Advisory

      An undeclared Tier 2 use discovered and corrected before the showcase; a Quizzitch Level 1 advisory. Recorded, no deduction.

      Level 2 · Deduction

      An undeclared Tier 2 use discovered at or after the showcase: 3–10 points from Script & Dramaturgy. A Quizzitch Level 2 finding: 5–20 marks from that candidate.

      Level 3 · Disqualification

      Any Tier 3 use; a confirmed Quizzitch Level 3 breach; misrepresentation of authorship in the defence. The delegation is disqualified and forfeits awards.

      Decorum · 5.4

      Immediate stage disqualification as set out in 5.4, decided by the jury chair in the moment and final.

      5.10 Grievance protocol

      1. A delegation may contest a Level 2 or Level 3 finding, other than a 5.4 stage disqualification, by a written statement from the advisor within 60 minutes of being informed on the day, or within 48 hours for a Quizzitch finding.
      2. The finding is reviewed by an Integrity Panel of three organisers who did not make it. The delegation may present drafts, rehearsal recordings or other evidence and may be heard for up to ten minutes.
      3. The Panel confirms, reduces or overturns the finding and gives brief written reasons. Results affected by a pending review are announced only after the Panel decides.
      4. A 5.4 stage disqualification may be reviewed only on the ground that the wrong delegation or the wrong moment was identified. The judgment that content breached 5.4 is not open to appeal.
      The originality & decorum declaration

      Signed by every performer and the faculty advisor, and uploaded with the repertoire submission:

      1. Every word, joke, lyric and movement in our piece was written or devised by the members of our delegation, except the sources declared on our credits list.
      2. We have not used generative AI to write, rewrite or generate any part of our script, lyrics, music, voices or media.
      3. Our piece does not reproduce any existing sketch, routine, special, play or online video.
      4. Our performance, Curveball response and defence will observe the decorum and content code in R-05.4, and we understand that any breach ends our performance immediately.
      5. We will be able to explain how our piece was written and will keep our drafts until results are final.

      2. 7.2

      Section 06 · Evaluation rubrics & theatrical FAQ

      How the jury reads a performance

      Every finalist is scored by every juror on the same 100-point ballot. The bands below describe what each level of performance looks like, so that troupes can rehearse against the rubric rather than guess at it. Jurors place a performance in a band first, then choose a mark within the band.

      85–100%

      Exceptional

      Work that would hold its own on a professional fringe stage. Fully original, precisely executed, memorable.

      65–84%

      Proficient

      Strong, confident and coherent, with clear craft. Minor lapses in economy, timing or depth.

      40–64%

      Developing

      A real idea and visible effort, carried unevenly. Noticeable gaps in structure, delivery or focus.

      0–39%

      Limited

      The criterion is barely addressed, or execution prevents the audience from following the piece.

      06.1Theatrical FAQ · for performers, directors & advisors

      A · Creative choices

      Q01

      Comedy or serious drama: which does the jury prefer?

      Neither. The rubric does not reward a tone; it rewards the right tone for the idea, executed well. A quiet, moving piece on track T-II and a sharp stand-up set on track T-I can both reach the Exceptional band. What the jury does notice is a mismatch: a serious premise undercut by nervous jokes, or a comic premise that stops to deliver a sermon.

      If you are torn, tragicomedy is often the strongest choice for a ten-minute slot. Laughter lowers the audience's guard; the serious moment then lands harder. Decide which feeling you want the house to leave with, and build backwards from the final minute.

      Q02

      Can we mix forms, for example stand-up with dance?

      Yes, and many strong pieces do. Declare the principal form on the Repertoire Declaration and list the others in your synopsis. The test the jury applies is whether each form is doing work the others cannot. A movement sequence that shows what the stand-up could not say is strong; a dance inserted because one member dances well is not.

      Plan transitions between forms carefully in the cue sandbox. Changes of form often need a change of light and sound, and those seconds count.

      Q03

      Can we satirise the news, real events, or school life?

      You may satirise ideas, habits, systems and situations drawn from real life: exam culture, media framing, the queue at a government office, the corporate launch event. You may not portray, name or imitate an identifiable real person, including a teacher, principal, classmate, public figure or juror, and you may not campaign for or against a political party or leader (R-05.4, R-05.5).

      The practical technique is the one great satirists have always used: invent a fictional character or organisation that embodies the habit you are targeting. The audience will recognise the habit; nobody will be able to say you mocked a person.

      Q04

      How original is "original"? We love a famous sketch format.

      Forms and structures are free for everyone: the courtroom scene, the news bulletin, the job interview, the double act, the parody advertisement. Content is not. If you take a well-known format, every line, character and turn inside it must be yours, and the jury will expect you to have done something with the format that its best-known versions did not.

      Re-enacting or closely imitating a specific sketch, special or video, even with new names, is a Tier 3 breach. When in doubt, ask yourself whether a juror who has seen the original would say "that is their version of that sketch". If so, write something else.

      Q05

      Can one performer carry most of the piece?

      Roles can be unequal, and a strong central performer is an asset. But every registered performer must appear on stage in the performance and in the Curveball, and Ensemble Synergy is part of a 25-point criterion. A monologue with three silent bystanders will struggle. Give every member a function the piece cannot do without, even if it is small: a narrator, a foil, a chorus, a physical role.

      B · Stage, sound & technical

      Q06

      Can we bring musical instruments?

      Yes. Acoustic instruments (guitar, violin, flute, harmonium, tabla, dholak, cajón, percussion) are welcome, and one keyboard or electric instrument can use the single AUX/DI input. Drum kits, personal amplifiers and powered speakers are not permitted. Instruments and cases must fit the one-trunk rule, or be carried and declared on the prop manifest if they are too large for the trunk but can be held by a performer (for example a guitar in a soft case).

      Tuning and setup happen inside the 1:30 bump-in, so tune in the green room and keep setup simple. Live playing counts as live performance; there is no limit on how much of your piece is live music.

      Q07

      Can we use digital backing slides or video?

      Where the venue allows projection (confirmed in the Technical Rider), you may submit one PDF of up to 20 slides or MP4 video with a total running time of up to 60 seconds, run by the AEROSS crew on your cues. Projection should support the performance, not replace it: a backdrop, a location card, a mock broadcast, a single image that changes the meaning of a scene.

      Projected text may not contain your script, lyrics or cue notes; that would be a prompter (Tier 3). AI-generated images or video are not permitted in submitted media. Plan a version of your piece that works without projection too, in case of an equipment failure.

      Q08

      How do microphones work with five performers?

      There are four cordless lapel microphones and two cordless handhelds on stands at DSR and DSL. With five performers, decide which four wear lapels (usually the members with the most lines) and give the fifth the handheld moments, or write the fifth role around unamplified projection or physical work.

      Lapels are fitted by the crew during bump-in, clipped near the collarbone. Avoid costume that rustles against the microphone, necklaces, and hugging scenes that crush the capsule. Handhelds should be held about a fist's width from the mouth and never pointed at the speakers. Mark in your cue sheet any moment a handheld passes between performers.

      Q09

      Can we use recorded music?

      Yes, within the integrity tiers. Freely usable: original music recorded by your members, instrumental tracks you are licensed to use, and public-domain compositions. With declaration: open-licence sound effects and a parody lyric set to a named melody. Not permitted: pre-recorded lead vocals, lip-sync, or AI-generated music or voices. Brief quotation of a published recording within an original piece is a Tier 2 use limited to 30 seconds in total.

      Submit each track as a separate file named by cue number, and write a clear trigger for each on the cue sheet ("SQ03 GO on the line 'Houston, we have a queue'").

      Q10

      How do our props get to the stage and back?
      1. Pack everything into one carry-on trunk (at most 56 × 45 × 25 cm, 12 kg) and list every item on the prop manifest.
      2. At accreditation, the trunk is opened and checked against the manifest and the prohibited list; it is then tagged and held backstage.
      3. At standby, the trunk is brought to the stage-left wing. During the 1:30 bump-in, performers set their props.
      4. During the 1:00 strike, everything returns to the trunk, which is collected by the advisor.

      Chairs and a table can be requested on the cue sheet and are set by the crew. Anything that cannot be set in 1:30 by your own members is too complicated for this stage.

      Q11

      What about costumes and make-up?

      Costumes worn by performers do not count towards the trunk. Keep them simple enough for quick changes in the wings if your piece needs them, and safe to move in, with secure footwear and nothing trailing. Make-up and costume must never caricature any race, ethnicity, caste, region or community (R-05.4). Masks are welcome if they do not block projection of the voice, or if a lapel is placed accordingly.

      C · The Curveball & the defence

      Q12

      How should we use the 60-second huddle?

      Sixty seconds is not enough to write anything, so do not try. Use it to agree three things:

      1. The one idea. What single transformation does the prompt make to your world? Say it in one sentence.
      2. The opener. Who starts, where on stage, and with what first line or action.
      3. The lander. Who is responsible for finding the ending, and what signal tells everyone it is coming.

      Everything else is built live by accepting and extending each other's offers. Troupes that rehearse this three-point huddle regularly using the drill in Section 04.4 consistently perform better than troupes that improvise the huddle itself.

      Q13

      Can we prepare for the Curveball?

      You can prepare the method, not the content. The six categories are published so you can practise each one: genre shifts, technical constraints, perspective reversals, audience conditions, time displacements and stakes inversions. The prompts themselves are written by the jury, sealed, and opened only on stage.

      Performing a prepared bit that ignores the prompt scores in the Limited band. Knowing your own characters deeply is the best preparation: a troupe that knows how each character would react to anything can answer any prompt.

      Q14

      What happens in the Artistic Intent defence?

      For five minutes, the jurors ask the troupe about its piece: why this structure, why that pause, what the ending means, why this track, how a particular scene was written and by whom, what was cut and why. It is a conversation between practitioners, not an examination. Any performer may answer; the advisor may not.

      Prepare by being able to explain, in plain words, what your piece is about, what it wants the audience to feel or think, and one choice you made that you are proud of and one you argued about. Honest answers about difficult choices impress juries far more than rehearsed speeches.

      D · Quizzitch, timing & logistics

      Q15

      Do performers really need to study for Quizzitch?

      In qualifying mode, the Composite Team Index decides who reaches the stage, so yes. In direct mode it decides your seed and therefore your choice of performance slot. The syllabus in Section 03.3 is also, deliberately, useful for writing: the devices of satire, the structure of drama, the stories of exploration and the paradoxes of identity are raw material for your piece. Divide the six modules among your members, then teach each other.

      Q16

      What if one of our members cannot sit the paper?

      Before the window opens, the advisor may replace that member with another student from the same school under R-01.4, who then sits the paper. If the member remains registered and does not sit, they score 0, which lowers the delegation's mean (see the worked example in 03.4). At least two members must sit for the delegation to remain eligible.

      Q17

      Our piece runs 7:10 in rehearsal. What happens?

      At 7:10 on the day you would receive 3 of the 5 timing points (R-07.4), and the jury would likely feel the piece was thin. Performances also run differently in front of a live house: laughter adds time, nerves take it away. Aim for 8:30 to 9:15 in rehearsal, which leaves a safe margin on both sides. Build your cue sheet in the sandbox and check where your transitions and blackouts sit.

      Q18

      Can our faculty advisor help us write or direct?

      The advisor may give feedback, ask questions, supervise rehearsals and point out problems, as any teacher would. The advisor may not write lines, jokes, lyrics or choreography for the troupe, and may not direct during the showcase. Every change to the piece must be made by the performers themselves, and the defence will show whether they did.

      Q19

      A performer falls ill just before the showcase. What now?

      Inform the organisers through the advisor at once. Up to 48 hours before the showcase, a replacement from the same school may be approved for documented illness or emergency (R-01.4). After that, the delegation performs with the members present, provided at least two take the stage (R-01.5). The jury scores the piece as performed; there is no allowance for the missing member, so rehearse a reduced version as a contingency.

      Q20

      Can we perform in Hindi?

      Yes. Performances may be in English, Hindi or a mix of both, with brief passages in other Indian languages where the meaning is clear from context (R-01.7). The same decorum code applies in every language. The Quizzitch paper is in English, and the defence may be answered in English or Hindi.

      No questions match that search. Try a single word such as "mic", "props" or "time", or write to the organisers through the portal.
