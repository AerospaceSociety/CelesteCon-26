Section 01 · The brief

Imagine boldly.
Then prove it holds air.

Anyone can draw a beautiful ring in space. We are looking for the team that can tell us how thick its walls are, where its air comes from, and what happens when the lights go out.

What Settle-Me-This is

Settle-Me-This is the flagship engineering competition of CelesteCon, the aerospace and STEM conclave of AEROSS at Delhi Public School, R.K. Puram. Each division receives a formal Request for Proposal (RFP) from a fictional settlement authority. Teams respond with a structured technical proposal for a complete, permanently inhabited settlement: its structure, its environmental protection, its life support and power, the lives of the people inside it, the robots that build and maintain it, and the logistics and money that make it possible.

Why a settlement is the hardest design brief there is

A settlement is not one machine. It is a system of systems, and every system is coupled to every other one. Choose a lower cabin pressure and your hull gets thinner, but your fire rules, your cooking and your nitrogen imports all change. Grow more of your own food and you save launch mass, but you need more power, more radiator area and more floor space under shielding. Nothing can be designed in isolation, and nothing can be left out: there is no outside air to open a window to, no supermarket, no ambulance, no second chance.

Imagination, bound by physics

We want ambitious, beautiful, humane places to live. We also want every one of them to obey the same laws of physics that govern real spacecraft. Each RFP comes with a shared fact sheet and a list of mandatory calculations. There are no gravity plates, force fields or inertial dampeners. The creativity we reward is in how you use real constraints: turning a lava tube into a radiation shield, or a slow spin rate into a quieter, calmer town.

What you take away

Teams leave having written a real engineering proposal, having done trade studies that engineers would recognise, and, if shortlisted, having defended their numbers in front of working scientists. Finalists receive written comments from their Round 1 judges before they present.

01 · The physics test

No magic, anywhere

Gravity comes from mass or spin. Shielding comes from mass or distance. Heat leaves only by radiation. Your design either respects these laws or it is not scored as a design.

02 · The mass budget test

Every tonne has an origin

Everything in your settlement came from Earth, from local rock, or from recycling. The judges will ask which, how much, and at what cost. A settlement with no mass budget is a drawing.

03 · The enclosure test

Close the loops

Air, water, food, waste and heat must balance, day after day, with honest recycling efficiencies. Show us the flows, the losses, and where the make-up mass comes from.

04 · The cross-exam test

Write only what you can defend

In Round 2, any number in your proposal can be questioned. If you cannot derive it at the podium, do not put it in the PDF.

DESIGN HERITAGEWhat we borrowed, and from whom

Space Settlement Design Competition (SSDC)

The RFP discipline

SSDC teams act as aerospace contractors answering a detailed Request for Proposal. From it we take:

  • A numbered, requirement-driven proposal structure that judges can audit section by section.
  • Functional allocation: every requirement is assigned to a system that meets it.
  • Trade studies that compare options on stated criteria before a choice is made.
  • Operations, schedules and cost as first-class engineering content, not afterthoughts.

NSS Gerard K. O'Neill Space Settlement Contest

The settlement vision

The NSS contest asks students to imagine settlements people would actually want to live in. From it we take:

  • Artificial gravity physics and rotation comfort as core design drivers.
  • Radiation protection sized for whole lifetimes, including children.
  • Ecological viability: food, water and air as living, closing loops.
  • Community livability: architecture, green space, light and dignity.

AEROSS teams have competed at both of these international contests. Settle-Me-This is an independent AEROSS competition and is not affiliated with or endorsed by the organisers of SSDC or the NSS contest.

R1 + R2 = 200Final score · combined total

Your proposal score carries all the way to the final result

Finalists are ranked on the combined total of their Round 1 proposal score (out of 100) and their Round 2 defence score (out of 100). Round 1 does not reset at the finale. Deep research, rigorous calculation and careful writing keep their full weight in the final tally, so a team whose live presentation meets an anomaly (a nervous start, a projector fault, a curveball that lands badly) remains fully in contention on the strength of its engineering.

If two teams finish level, the higher Round 1 score ranks first. If they are still level, the higher score on the Round 1 structural engineering criterion decides. If they are still level, the jury chair's ranking of the two teams' calculations ledgers decides.

Junior DivisionGrades 6–8Moon · Marius Hills · 2045

Marius Hollow

Build a town inside an empty river of lava. 300 pioneers, 25 metres of rock overhead, and two weeks of night every month.

RFPSMT-JLunar Settlement Office

In the Moon's Ocean of Storms, among a cluster of low volcanic domes called the Marius Hills, there is a hole in the ground. It was spotted in 2009 by Japan's Kaguya spacecraft: a round pit about 65 metres wide and more than 80 metres deep. Scientists believe it is a skylight, a place where the roof of a lava tube has collapsed. Lava tubes are tunnels left behind when rivers of molten rock drained away billions of years ago. Some may be wide enough to hold a small town.

It is 2045. The Lunar Settlement Office (LSO), the agency that governs long-term human presence on the Moon, has decided to build humanity's first permanent town inside the Marius Hills tube. Robotic landers have already cleared a landing pad two kilometres from the skylight and lowered a survey rover into the tube. The rover's measurements are on the fact sheet below.

Why go underground? Because the Moon's surface is hostile. With no thick atmosphere and no global magnetic field, its surface is bathed in cosmic radiation and struck by tiny, fast micrometeoroids. The temperature swings from about 120 °C in sunlight to about −170 °C at night. Inside the tube, 25 metres of solid basalt overhead stops almost all of that. The temperature is steady. But the tube is dark, dusty and 85 metres below the surface, every tonne of cargo has to be lowered through the skylight, and on the surface above, the lunar night lasts about 354 hours.

The LSO wants a settlement for 300 pioneers: engineers, geologists, doctors, technicians, cooks, pilots and scientists who will live there for years at a time. Within ten years it must be able to grow to 500 people, including the first families. Your team is one of the design consortia invited to respond to RFP SMT-J.

The challenge

Design Marius Hollow. Decide how you will turn part of the tube into a safe, pressurised, livable town; how you will protect people who work on the surface; how you will supply air, water, food and power through the long lunar night; what a normal day inside feels like; and how robots and people will build it. Then prove, with numbers, that your settlement keeps 300 people alive and well.

The ground rules

  • Use the Marius Hollow Fact Sheet below for every value it covers. If you need a number that is not on it, use a real, cited figure or a clearly explained assumption, and record it in Section 7.1.
  • Use only technology that exists today or could reasonably exist by 2045. If you rely on something that has only been tested in a laboratory, say so and give a backup plan.
  • Everything that is not made on the Moon arrives by cargo lander, within the lander limits on the fact sheet.
  • People must be able to live in Marius Hollow permanently: design for at least ten years of operation, not a short camping trip.
  • No gravity plates, force fields, teleporters, food replicators or any other technology that breaks the laws of physics.

What a great entry looks like

A great Junior proposal reads like a plan the LSO could actually start building: a clear architecture chosen for good reasons, drawings with dimensions, a settlement that keeps people safe from radiation and the vacuum, air, water, food and power that add up, a daily life that people would enjoy, and eight calculations a judge can check line by line.

Choose your architecture

How will you hold the air in?

The tube protects you from radiation, but it is not airtight. Pick one primary architecture. You may combine ideas, but explain which one carries your population.

A
InflateLower folded soft-goods habitats through the skylight and inflate them on the tube floor. Light to ship and roomy, but they need anchoring, levelling and careful protection from sharp rock.
B
AssembleShip rigid metal modules from Earth and link them into a network. Proven and strong, but heavy, and each one must fit through the skylight and down the lift.
C
SealWall off a section of the tube itself, line it with an airtight membrane and pressurise the cavern. Enormous volume, but you must prove the liner and the end walls can hold the pressure.

Senior DivisionGrades 9–12Earth–Moon L5 · 2066

Halcyon

Four thousand people, turning twice a minute, 384,400 kilometres from home. Build it out of the Moon.

RFPSMT-SL5 Development Authority

In 1974 the physicist Gerard K. O'Neill argued that the best place for people to live in space might not be on a planet at all, but inside huge rotating structures built from lunar material. A year later, a NASA and Stanford University summer study (published as NASA SP-413) designed one in detail: the Stanford Torus, a wheel almost 1.8 kilometres across, home to 10,000 people at the Earth–Moon L5 point, shielded by millions of tonnes of lunar slag delivered by an electromagnetic mass driver.

It is 2066. The L5 Development Authority (LDA), a consortium of national space agencies, has spent a decade building the infrastructure that study imagined. A mass driver at a lunar highland mine now throws regolith to a catcher at L5. A smelter at L5 refines it into oxygen, aluminium, iron, silicon and glass, and a non-rotating construction station houses up to 600 workers. What does not yet exist is a place for people to live permanently.

The LDA has issued RFP SMT-S for Halcyon: a self-sustaining rotating settlement for 4,000 permanent residents plus up to 400 visitors. Its people will build solar power satellites and deep-space vehicles in the industrial yards nearby. They will be families. Children will be born and grow up there. The settlement must feel like a town, not a spacecraft.

L5 is a good address. It is gravitationally stable, bathed in constant sunlight, and equally distant from Earth and Moon. It is also a hard one. It lies far outside Earth's magnetic field, exposed to the full flux of galactic cosmic rays and solar storms. There is no gravity except what you spin up. And the Moon, your quarry, is poor in exactly the elements life depends on: nitrogen, carbon and hydrogen.

The challenge

Write the proposal that wins the Halcyon design contract. Choose your settlement's geometry through a documented trade study. Size its structure, rotation, shielding and atmosphere. Close its air, water and food loops with honest efficiencies. Power it and reject its heat. Design a community people would choose to raise children in. Specify the robots that build and maintain it. Then show how it gets built, phase by phase, within the LDA's mass, cost and schedule limits, and what happens when things go wrong.

The ground rules

  • Use the Halcyon Fact Sheet, materials table and hard requirements below. For anything they don't cover (crop varieties, specific equipment masses, dose models), use real, cited values.
  • Every technology must be demonstrated today at Technology Readiness Level 6 or above, or be a clearly justified extension of one. Anything less mature is allowed only with a stated fallback that also meets the requirements.
  • All hard requirements HR-1 to HR-12 must be met. A proposal that misses one must say so openly, quantify the shortfall and justify it; hiding a miss is scored far more harshly than admitting it.
  • Mass comes from three places only: Earth (expensive, capped), the Moon via the mass driver (cheap, but limited in elements and throughput), or lunar polar ice (water only). Nothing appears from nowhere.
  • No gravity plates, inertial dampeners, force fields, reactionless drives or room-temperature superconductors of convenience.

What a great entry looks like

A great Senior proposal reads like something the LDA's technical board could fund: a configuration chosen by weighted trade study rather than by taste, a structure whose wall thickness and mass follow from stated loads and allowables, closed loops with every loss accounted for, a power and thermal balance that agree with each other, a town with real human-factors reasoning, and a mass, cost and schedule that survive a risk register written by people who expect things to fail.

g = ω²rSpin gravity

Weight grows with radius and with the square of spin rate. Halve the radius and you must spin 41% faster for the same gravity.

224m minimum1 g at 2 rpm

2ωvm/s²Coriolis

Anything moving inside a spinning frame is pushed sideways. Walking is fine; a fast lift up a spoke is not.

Section 04 · Engineering proposal architecture

No blank template.
A blueprint instead.

Why there is no fill-in-the-blank template

Business competitions can hand out a template because most businesses share a shape: a product, a customer, a price, a cost. Space settlements do not. An orbital settlement and a lunar cave town share almost no answers, and even two designs for the same site can differ completely because one early decision changes everything after it.

A settlement is a system of systems. Form follows function: the shape of your hull comes from the gravity you want and the pressure you hold. Location dictates shielding: a lava tube gives you 7,500 g/cm² of free rock, while open space asks you to haul 4.5 tonnes of slag for every square metre. Population scale determines how closed your loops must be: at 300 people a leaky water system is an expensive nuisance; at 4,000 it is a supply chain you cannot afford.

A template with boxes to fill would push every team toward the same design and hide the reasoning we want to see. So instead we give you an architecture: the sections every proposal must have, what each must contain, the figures and tables the review board expects, the questions a good engineer asks at each step, and page guidance by division. How you fill it, in what depth, with what design, is yours.

A fill-in template would give you
  • Boxes sized for one kind of design
  • Answers that look alike across teams
  • No room for the trade studies that justify choices
  • A reason to stop thinking once a box is full
This architecture gives you
  • A fixed numbering the jury can audit, section by section
  • Required content and figures, not required answers
  • Guiding questions that expose the couplings
  • Division-specific depth and page guidance

R-05 · Read in full before you start

Originality & AI use

Applies to Round 1 and Round 2

This competition tests your team's own engineering judgement. The review board and the jury must be able to trust that the design, the words, the numbers, the drawings and the models in your proposal and your presentation are yours. This rule is deliberately specific so that no team can be unsure what is allowed. If a use of a tool is not clearly listed as allowed below, treat it as not allowed, or ask the organisers in writing before the deadline.

5.1What counts as "AI"

For this competition, AI means any software that generates, rewrites, completes, translates, summarises, designs or suggests content (text, numbers, tables, code, drawings, 3D geometry, images, audio, video or slides) instead of simply recording, displaying or calculating exactly what you typed, drew, modelled or entered. This includes AI built into everyday apps, whether or not the feature is labelled "AI".

It counts as using AI whether you typed a prompt, clicked a button such as "Rewrite", "Improve", "Summarise", "Generate", "Magic Write" or "Help me write", accepted a suggested phrase, let a feature autocomplete your text, or let a CAD tool generate or optimise geometry for you. Not knowing that a feature uses AI is not a defence. Each team is responsible for knowing what its tools do.

Robots, autonomy and "AI" as part of your settlement design (for example, an autonomous maintenance drone) are engineering content and are welcome. This rule is about the tools you use to produce your proposal and presentation.

5.3The three-tier integrity model

Tier 1 · Permitted standard tools

Ordinary tools that record, draw, calculate or check your own work. No declaration needed beyond listing major software in 7.4.

  • CAD and 3D modelling (for example Fusion, SolidWorks, Onshape, FreeCAD, SketchUp, Blender) where your team creates every sketch, feature and dimension, with generative and AI features switched off or unused.
  • Deterministic engineering tools: FEA solvers, CFD, orbit calculators, unit converters and the calculator on this page, run on models and inputs your team set up.
  • Native word processing and layout, with AI features switched off or unused.
  • Basic spelling and grammar check, accepted one misspelled word or punctuation mark at a time.
  • Formula spreadsheets, using formulas your team wrote.
  • Manual web research: ordinary search results (a list of links) and reading the original sources.
  • Citation managers that format sources you found yourselves.
  • Diagram tools (for example draw.io) where your team places every element; credited, non-AI stock images.
Tier 2 · Permitted with mandatory disclosure

Limited research help. Every use must be recorded in the Section 7.4 disclosure ledger: tool, date, what you asked, and how you used the answer.

  • Background conceptual research: asking an AI chatbot to explain a concept (for example, "what is hoop stress?") that you then study from a real source. You may not copy or paraphrase its answer.
  • Research paper discovery: using an AI chatbot or AI search to find papers and reports, as long as you open, read and cite the original, never the AI tool, for every fact you use.
  • Spreadsheet formula troubleshooting: asking why a formula your team wrote returns an error, or how a spreadsheet or CAD function works.
  • Asking an AI tool to check a calculation your team has already completed. The method, model and numbers must be your own.
Tier 3 · Strictly prohibited

Any use in which AI produces, rewrites, designs or decides part of your submission or presentation.

  • AI generating designs: settlement concepts, configurations, layouts, floor plans, trade study criteria or scores, risk registers, names or strategy.
  • AI writing sections: any text in the proposal, slides or script, including a single sentence, heading, caption or table entry; and any AI rewriting, "improving", summarising, expanding or translating of text your team wrote.
  • AI producing engineering numbers: asking AI for values, sizes, efficiencies, yields or results and using them, or letting it build your calculations. AI-supplied figures are frequently invented ("hallucinated") and are treated as fabricated data.
  • AI creating slide decks or slide layouts, including auto-designed templates.
  • AI generating CAD or renderings: text-to-3D, generative design, AI rendering, AI images, AI-edited photographs or AI-drawn diagrams.
  • Using humanizer, paraphrasing or detection-avoidance tools on any text. This is treated as deliberate concealment.
  • Using any AI tool or connected device during the Round 2 presentation, cross-examination or curveball.

5.5Plagiarism and help from others

  1. a
    Copying or closely paraphrasing text, tables, figures, drawings or data from any source without quotation marks and a citation is plagiarism. Short direct quotations are allowed in quotation marks with a citation.
  2. b
    Reusing another team's work, a previous year's entry, a published SSDC or NSS contest entry, or a published settlement design (including NASA SP-413) as your own is plagiarism, even if it has been changed. Reference designs may be cited and compared, never presented as yours.
  3. c
    A team member's own earlier work (for example, a previous contest entry) may be reused only if current team members created it, it is cited, and it is declared in 7.4. The proposal must still be substantially new work answering this RFP.
  4. d
    Teachers, parents, mentors and professionals may discuss ideas, ask questions and give general feedback. They may not write, rewrite, edit, calculate, draw, model or design any part of the proposal or slides. Significant feedback must be described in 7.4.
  5. e
    Paid or professional services (writers, consultants, CAD modellers, designers, tutors producing content) are not allowed.

TECHNICAL FAQFrequently asked

Eligibility & teams
Q01Who can take part, and which division do we enter?

Any student in Grades 6–12 at a recognised school in India can take part, in a team of 2–4 students from the same school, with a teacher from that school as faculty advisor.

Junior is for Grades 6–8 and Senior is for Grades 9–12. If your team includes students from both ranges, you must enter the Senior Division. Each student's grade on the Round 1 deadline is what counts.

Q02How many teams can one school enter?

There is no fixed limit. Each student can be on only one team, and each team's work must be completely independent. Sharing ideas, text, calculations, drawings or CAD models between teams from the same school is treated as plagiarism under Rule R-05.

The challenge & design
Q03Can we choose a different site from the RFP?

Only through a documented site-selection trade study in Section 1.3, and only if the new site serves the same RFP. Junior teams may, for example, argue for another lunar pit such as those in Mare Tranquillitatis or Mare Ingenii; Senior teams may argue for L4 or another orbit.

If you move, every value that differs from the fact sheet (radiation, temperatures, distances, delivery costs) must come from a cited source and be recorded in 7.1. You are scored on the same rubric, and the jury will test whether the move was worth it. Most teams will do best by using the baseline site well.

Q04Can we change a number on the fact sheet?

Fact sheet numbers are the default so everyone starts from the same ground. You may replace one only with a better real-world source: cite it, record old and new values in 7.1, and show how it changes your results. You may not invent new scenario facts, such as extra lander capacity or cheaper launch.

Q05What if our calculations show the design does not quite work?

Say so. An honest proposal that identifies a shortfall, quantifies it and proposes a mitigation scores far better than one that hides a problem the jury will find anyway. Record it in the compliance summary (0.3) and your risk register (6.5).

Q06How much real engineering do we need?

Junior teams need the eight calculations, correct physics for their site, and clear reasoning. No calculus is required: every Junior formula uses multiplication, division, square roots and π.

Senior teams should work at the level of a first-year university engineering trade study: stated loads, allowables and safety factors, closed mass balances, and consistency checks. Thin-wall and ideal-gas approximations are acceptable if stated.

Tools, AI & help
Q07Which software can we use for diagrams and CAD?

Any CAD, diagramming, drawing or spreadsheet software, free or paid, as long as your team creates every element and any AI or generative features are switched off or unused (Tier 1 under R-05). Examples: Fusion, Onshape, FreeCAD, SolidWorks, SketchUp, Blender, draw.io, Inkscape, LibreOffice and spreadsheet tools. List the software you used in Section 7.4.

Q08Are hand-drawn sketches acceptable, or must we use CAD?

Hand-drawn figures are fully acceptable and are scored on the same basis as CAD: clarity, labels, dimensions and a scale. Scan or photograph them at good resolution, straighten them, and caption them like any other figure. A carefully dimensioned hand section often beats a pretty render with no numbers.

Q09Can we use AI tools like ChatGPT or Gemini?

Only in the limited, declared ways listed in Tier 2 of Rule R-05: understanding a concept, discovering research papers you then read and cite yourselves, and troubleshooting spreadsheet formulas or checking arithmetic you have already done. Every such use goes in Section 7.4.

AI must not generate your design, write or rewrite your text, supply engineering numbers, build your slides, or generate CAD models or renderings. This includes AI features inside Word, Google Docs, Grammarly, Canva and CAD tools.

Q10How do we reference research papers and NASA reports?

Use APA 7th edition in Section 7.3 and cite in the text where you use each fact. NASA technical reports (for example on the NASA Technical Reports Server) are cited as reports with their document number, such as NASA SP-413. Journal articles include the DOI where there is one.

Cite the paper you actually read, not a summary of it. If you could not access a paywalled paper, cite the source where you found its figure, and say so. Never cite an AI tool as a source of facts.

Q11Can our teacher, parents or a scientist help us?

They can coach: discuss ideas, ask hard questions, point out weak spots and suggest what to read. They cannot write, edit, calculate, draw, model or design any part of your proposal or slides. Describe significant feedback in Section 7.4. Your advisor countersigns the declaration.

Q12Can we reuse an entry we made for SSDC, NSS or another contest?

Your own earlier work may be cited and built on if current team members created it and you declare it in 7.4 (R-05.5c). The proposal must still be substantially new work answering this RFP and this fact sheet. Resubmitting an old entry, or anyone else's, is plagiarism.

Submission & format
Q13What counts toward the page limit?

Sections 1.0 to 6.0, including every figure and table inside them: up to 18 pages for Junior and 30 for Senior. Front matter (0.0), Section 7.0, the declaration and appendices do not count, but Section 7.2 is capped at 8 pages (Junior) or 12 pages (Senior).

Q14Should we work in dollars or rupees?

The Senior fact sheet is in 2066 US dollars, and working in dollars is simplest. If you prefer rupees, state the exchange rate, its source and its date once in 7.1, and use it consistently. Junior teams do not need to cost their settlement unless they choose to.

Q15Can we update our proposal after uploading it?

Yes, as many times as you like until the deadline; only your latest upload counts. Nothing can be changed after the deadline. Upload a complete version at least a day early, then replace it if you improve it.

Round 2 & results
Q16How does travel to Round 2 work, and what if one of us can't attend?

Finalists present in person at DPS R.K. Puram, New Delhi. Travel, accommodation and supervision are arranged by the participating school with parents or guardians (R-10.3); the organisers publish venue, reporting times and schedule to finalist advisors in advance.

At least two team members must attend and present. Members who cannot attend remain part of the team and share its result. Remote participation is not offered.

Q17What happens in the curveball?

During cross-examination your team draws one sealed scenario card: a failure, a delay or an emergency in your settlement. You get a 60-second huddle, then up to 3 minutes to respond. The jury wants your first action, the numbers from your own design that matter, and how you recover. See the sample cards in Section 03.

Q18If our presentation goes badly, can we still win?

Yes. The final ranking is Round 1 plus Round 2, out of 200, and your proposal score carries forward in full. A rigorous proposal continues to count even if the live presentation does not go to plan.

Q19How are ties broken?

First by the higher Round 1 score. If still level, by the higher score on the Round 1 structural criterion (Structure & environmental protection for Junior; Structural engineering & rotational dynamics for Senior). If still level, by the jury chair's ranking of the two teams' calculations ledgers.

Q20Where do we find deadlines, and who do we contact?

Deadlines and the finale date are on the CelesteCon portal, published at registration and emailed to faculty advisors, all in IST. If an official update differs from this page, the later official update applies.

Questions go from your faculty advisor to the organisers in writing, using the contact details on the portal. Ask about the rules, especially AI use, before the Round 1 deadline.
