Section 01 · The brief

Imagine boldly.
Count every gram.

Anyone can imagine a café on the Moon. We are looking for the team that can tell us what a cup of tea costs there, and why people will pay for it.

What Business Power Pitch is

Business Power Pitch is the business and entrepreneurship competition of CelesteCon, the aerospace and STEM conclave of AEROSS at Delhi Public School, R.K. Puram. Teams design a company from scratch, write it up as a structured business proposal, and, if shortlisted, pitch it live to a panel of judges from industry, finance and aerospace who will question every decision.

Why this year's challenges are set beyond Earth

Ordinary business plans can lean on shortcuts: a supplier down the road, cheap delivery, millions of possible customers. An off-world setting takes those away. There is no supermarket to restock from. Every kilogram of stock carries a shipping price. Customers can be counted by name, and the environment itself changes how a product works. That makes it impossible to skip the fundamentals: who the customer is, what things really cost, how the operation runs day to day, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Imaginative setting, strict rules

Each challenge comes with a shared fact sheet of prices and conditions, and with real physics that every team must respect. There are no teleporters, food replicators or free energy. The creativity we want to see is in how you use the real constraints: turning low gravity into a product, or a 26-month supply gap into a strategy.

What you gain

Teams leave having built a complete business model, practised defending it under questioning, and received feedback from professionals. Finalists get written comments from the Round 1 judges before they pitch.

The gram test

Mass is money

Every kilogram that leaves Earth has a price on the fact sheet. If your plan ignores it, the judges will not.

The physics test

The planet is a line item

Gravity, cabin pressure, dust, darkness and signal delay change how your product works and what it costs. Use them. Don't wish them away.

The customer test

Name who pays

Your market is a few hundred people with a fixed monthly budget. Show who buys, how often, and why they choose you over doing nothing.

The cross-exam test

Write what you can defend

In Round 2, any number in your proposal can be questioned. If you can't explain where it came from, leave it out.

WHAT THE JUDGES REWARDFour things that win

An idea that belongs there

A business that only makes sense in its setting and uses the environment as a feature, not just an obstacle.

Numbers that hold together

Costs, prices and sales that agree with each other and with the fact sheet, with every calculation shown.

Junior DivisionGrades 6–8Moon · South Pole · 2042

Moonside

Open for business at the bottom of the Moon. 160 people, one free canteen, and nowhere to spend a Saturday.

ChallengeBPP-JShackleton Village

Shackleton Village sits on the rim of Shackleton Crater at the Moon's south pole. The rim catches sunlight for most of the lunar month, while the crater floor below stays in permanent shadow, cold enough to have held water ice for billions of years. That ice is why the Village exists: it gives the settlement drinking water, breathable oxygen and rocket fuel.

It is 2042. About 160 people live in the Village on any given day. Most are crew: engineers, geologists, doctors, cooks and technicians on six-month shifts, working eight hours a day, six days a week. The rest are tourists. Every ten days a passenger shuttle lands with 40 visitors who have paid a fortune for a ten-day stay, and leaves with the 40 whose trip has ended.

The Village Authority, which runs the settlement, feeds everyone for free. The food is nutritionally perfect, comes vacuum-packed as pastes and bars in twenty flavours, and is so boring that the crew have started rating each flavour out of ten. The Village doctor's latest report says morale is dropping. People miss ordinary things: a hot snack after a shift, somewhere to play, something to buy for their families back home.

To fix this, the Authority has opened the Village's first commercial arcade: twelve pressurised units along the main corridor between the crew quarters and the tourist lodge. Two are already taken. Your team has won the lease for Unit 7. An Earth-based investor, Lunar Ventures, has agreed to fund your start-up costs up to $500,000, as long as the money is paid back within three years of opening.

The challenge

Design the business that goes into Unit 7. Decide what you sell, who buys it and why, how it works on the Moon, and what it costs to run. Then prove, with numbers, that it covers its monthly costs and pays back the investor within 36 months.

The ground rules

  • Use the Village Fact Sheet below for all prices and conditions. If you need a number that isn't on it, use a real, cited figure or a clearly explained assumption.
  • Your business must fit in one arcade unit (10–40 m²) and follow the Village rules: no open flames, no loose crumbs or powders, and no alcohol, tobacco, gambling or weapons.
  • Everything must work with technology that exists today or could reasonably exist by 2042. No teleporters, food replicators or free energy.
  • Your customers are the Village's crew and tourists. You cannot sell to Earth unless you pay to ship the product there.

What a great entry looks like

A great Junior proposal reads like a plan a Village resident could believe in: a clear idea that only makes sense on the Moon, customers who feel like real people, prices that make sense next to a crew member's monthly budget, and maths a judge can check line by line.

Choose one lane

What goes in Unit 7?

Pick one lane. Any concept inside it is allowed. The best ideas use something about the Moon that Earth can't offer.

B

C
Shop & ServiceSouvenirs, personalised gifts, a repair counter, a laundry, a postal service. Tourists want to take something home, but every kilogram sent back to Earth costs $20,000, so small, light and meaningful wins.

How to use the fact sheet

Use these numbers by default. Every team works from the same figures so judges can compare fairly. Cite them as "BPP-26 Fact Sheet J".

Missing a number? Use a real figure with a citation, or a clearly explained assumption. Record it in your assumption ledger (6.1).

Changing a number? Allowed only with a cited real-world source. Show the old value, the new value and how it changes your results.

Don't invent new facts about the Village, such as free power, extra cargo space or a subsidy. They will be treated as errors.

Include
  • Named customers. Real-feeling profiles: who they are, what they earn, what they miss about Earth.

  • Copied text from websites, other teams or AI tools. See Rule R-05.

Senior DivisionGrades 9–12Mars · Arcadia Planitia · 2049

The 26-Month Pantry

Feed a Martian settlement above the ration line, with one supply window every 26 months and no room for a bad bet.

ChallengeBPP-SArcadia Station

Arcadia Station sits on the ice-rich plains of Arcadia Planitia in Mars's northern mid-latitudes, where orbiting radar has found water ice a metre or two beneath the dust. The ice is why the Station is here: it supplies drinking water, oxygen, and hydrogen for rocket fuel.

It is January 2049. The Station is home to 240 settlers, mostly engineers, technicians and scientists working ten-hour shifts, six sols on and one off. A crew ship arriving with the next transfer window will bring the population to about 400, and the plan is 600 by late 2052, when the first families with children arrive.

The Settlement Authority guarantees every settler 2,800 kilocalories a sol of shelf-stable rations and three litres of drinking water. The rations keep people healthy. They do not keep them here. In the Authority's last morale survey, food ranked second among the reasons settlers gave for wanting to leave, behind only distance from family. Settlers can keep a small herb planter in their cabin and receive a 2 kg parcel from home each window. That is the entire food economy above the ration line.

The Authority is now issuing one five-year commercial licence for food and drink above the ration line, and your company has been shortlisted for it. The licence gives you up to 400 m² of pressurised greenhouse space, 8 tonnes of pre-booked cargo on the first window, access to settler labour, and the right to sell to every settler at the Station. It also comes with a Price Board that caps margins on essential foods, a cargo schedule that opens only every 26 months, and a 5% chance that any cargo lander never arrives.

The challenge

Write the proposal that wins the licence. Decide which products you ship from Earth, which you grow or brew at Arcadia, and which you don't sell at all. Plan your inventory across supply windows, price your products for a closed economy of a few hundred customers, fund the business, and show that it survives the worst sol it is likely to face across a 60-month plan.

The ground rules

  • Use the Station Fact Sheet for all prices, schedules and rules. For anything it doesn't cover (crop yields, equipment power, food mass per serving), use real, cited values.
  • Rely only on technology demonstrated on Earth or in space today, or a clearly justified extension of it. Anything less mature needs a fallback plan.
  • Your plan runs from Month 1 to Month 60. Cargo bookings, arrivals and population changes must follow the Fact Sheet schedule.
  • No alcohol, tobacco or gambling. All biological production must be sealed and follow the Station's planetary-protection rules.
  • Land cannot be owned (Outer Space Treaty, Article II). Your greenhouse space is leased.

What a great entry looks like

A great Senior proposal reads like something the Authority's licensing board could approve: a distinctive concept grounded in what settlers value, a ship/grow/skip decision justified with numbers, a cash-flow plan that survives a lost lander and a dust storm, and every figure traceable to the Fact Sheet or a cited source.

The core decision

Ship it, grow it, or skip it?

For every product line, compare the landed Earth cost per kilogram with the full local cost per kilogram, including equipment you have to ship, power, water, floor space and labour. Where do they cross over?

↗

⌂

How to use the fact sheet

Use these numbers by default. Tariffs, rates, schedules, the Price Board and the Ledger are competition fictions so every team works in the same economy. Cite them as "BPP-26 Fact Sheet S".

Missing a number? Crop yields, equipment power draw, food mass per serving and similar values must come from real, cited sources, recorded in your assumption ledger (6.1).

Changing a number? Only with a cited source, stating the old value, the new value and the effect on your results.

Don't invent Station facts such as extra cargo, cheaper power or new subsidies. They will be treated as errors.

What to include · what to leave outSenior

Include
  • Unit economics by product line: price, variable cost, contribution margin, with the tariff built into every Earth-sourced unit.
  • A CapEx / OpEx split that reconciles: shipped mass × tariff for equipment, monthly operating costs clearly labelled.
  • An inventory model across the window cycle, with safety stock and carrying cost.
  • Sensitivity analysis on your two most uncertain inputs, showing what happens to break-even.
  • A risk register with likelihood, impact, owner and trigger for each mitigation.
  • A regulatory map citing the actual instruments: Outer Space Treaty (1967) Articles II and VI, Liability Convention (1972), COSPAR planetary protection policy, Artemis Accords, and your authorising state's process (for India, IN-SPACe under the Indian Space Policy 2023).
  • An assumption ledger: every number, its source and your confidence in it.
Leave out
  • Unproven technology presented as ready. If something isn't demonstrated today, say so and show your de-risking plan and fallback.
  • Revenue from nowhere. Asteroid mining, exports to Earth or "tourism later" that the Fact Sheet can't support.
  • Hockey-stick curves that ignore a population cap of 600 and a budget of $2,000 a month.
  • Hand-waved balance sheets. Totals that don't reconcile, missing shipping on CapEx, OpEx with no basis.
  • Ignoring time. Light delay, the conjunction blackout, 18-month booking lead times.
  • Land ownership claims. Article II of the Outer Space Treaty rules out national appropriation. Work with leases.
  • Plagiarism or AI-generated content. Grounds for disqualification. See Rule R-05.

Originality & AI use

Applies to Round 1 and Round 2

This competition tests your team's own thinking. Judges must be able to trust that the ideas, words, numbers and images in your proposal and pitch are yours. This rule is deliberately specific so that no team can be unsure what is allowed. If a use of a tool is not clearly listed as allowed below, treat it as not allowed, or ask the organisers in writing before the deadline.

5.1What counts as "AI"

For this competition, AI means any software that generates, rewrites, completes, translates, summarises or suggests content (text, numbers, tables, code, images, audio, video or slides) instead of simply recording, displaying or calculating exactly what you typed, drew or entered. This includes AI built into everyday apps, whether or not the feature is labelled "AI".

It counts as using AI whether you typed a prompt, clicked a button such as "Rewrite", "Improve", "Summarise", "Magic Write" or "Help me write", accepted a suggested phrase or sentence, or let a feature autocomplete your text. Not knowing that a feature uses AI is not a defence. Each team is responsible for knowing what its tools do.

5.3What is allowed, what must be declared, what is prohibited

Tier 1 · Not AI use

Ordinary tools that record or check your own work. No declaration needed.

  • Typing, drawing and formatting in a word processor, spreadsheet or slide app, with its AI features switched off or unused.
  • Automatic correction of individual misspelled words and basic punctuation, accepted one word or mark at a time.
  • Spreadsheets and calculators, using formulas your team wrote.
  • Ordinary web search (a list of links) and reading the original sources you find.
  • Citation tools (for example Zotero or Word's citation manager) that format sources you found yourselves.
  • Diagram and design tools (for example draw.io, or Canva without its Magic features) where your team creates every element.
  • Stock photos and icons from libraries, credited in the caption. They must not be AI-generated.
Tier 2 · Allowed only if declared

Limited research help. Every use must be listed in Section 6.3: the tool, the date, what you asked, and how you used the answer.

  • Asking an AI chatbot to explain a concept you then research further (for example, "what is contribution margin?"). You may not copy or paraphrase its answer into your proposal.
  • Using an AI chatbot or AI search to find sources, as long as you open, read and cite the original source, never the AI tool, for every fact you use.
  • Asking an AI tool to check a calculation your team has already done. The method, model and numbers must be your own.
  • Asking an AI tool how to use a spreadsheet function or software feature.
Tier 3 · Prohibited

Any use in which AI produces, rewrites or decides part of your submission or pitch.

  • AI writing any text in the proposal, slides or pitch script, including a single sentence, heading, caption, slogan or table entry.
  • AI rephrasing, rewriting, "improving", shortening, expanding, summarising or changing the tone of text your team wrote, including Grammarly or Word suggestions that rewrite phrases or sentences.
  • AI translating text your team wrote in another language into English.
  • AI generating or suggesting your business idea, names, brand, customer profiles, SWOT, risks or strategy.
  • AI building your financial model, choosing numbers or assumptions, or producing tables or charts.
  • AI-generated or AI-edited images, logos, mock-ups, diagrams or illustrations.
  • AI-generated or AI-designed slide decks or layouts.
  • Using "humanizer", paraphrasing or detection-avoidance tools on any text. This is treated as deliberate concealment.
  • Using any AI tool or connected device during the Round 2 pitch or Q&A.

Section 06 · Questions

Frequently asked.

Who can take part, and which division do we enter?

Any student in Grades 6–12 at a recognised school in India can take part, in a team of 2–4 students from the same school, with a teacher from that school as faculty advisor.

Junior is for Grades 6–8 and Senior is for Grades 9–12. If your team includes students from both ranges (for example, one Grade 8 and one Grade 9 student), you must enter the Senior Division. Each student's grade on the Round 1 deadline is what counts.

How many teams can one school enter?

There is no fixed limit per school. Each student can be on only one team, and each team's work must be completely independent.

Two teams from the same school sharing ideas, text, calculations or figures is treated as plagiarism under Rule R-05, so keep your work separate from other teams at your school.

Can we set our business somewhere other than the scenario?

No. Each division has one world so that judges can compare every entry fairly. Your creative freedom lives inside that world.

Junior teams choose a lane (Eat & Drink, Play & Entertainment, or Shop & Service) and any concept within it. Senior teams choose their product lines, their ship/grow/skip mix and their business model. A proposal set somewhere else will score poorly on premise and feasibility.

Can we change a number on the Fact Sheet?

Fact Sheet numbers are the default so that every team starts from the same ground. You may replace one only if you have a better real-world source. If you do, cite the source, record the old and new values in your assumption ledger (6.1), and show how the change affects your results.

You may not invent new facts about the settlement, such as free power, extra cargo space or a subsidy. If the Fact Sheet doesn't cover something you need (for example, how much flour goes into a loaf), use a cited real-world figure or a clearly explained assumption.

Should we work in dollars or rupees?

The Fact Sheets are in US dollars, and working in dollars is simplest. If you prefer rupees, state the exchange rate you used, its source and its date once in Section 6.1, convert every Fact Sheet figure with it, and use it consistently throughout.

Ignore taxes and inflation unless you choose to model them. If you do, explain your method in 6.1.

How much real science do we need?

Enough that your product would actually work under the conditions listed for your world. You are not expected to be engineers, but you are expected not to ignore physics.

Junior teams should show they understand at least two physical effects and what they change about the business. Senior teams should calculate the resource needs of their operation (power, water, floor space and mass) using real, cited figures for things like crop yields, equipment power draw and food per serving.

Can we use AI tools like ChatGPT or Gemini?

Only in the limited, declared ways listed in Tier 2 of Rule R-05: understanding a concept, finding sources that you then read and cite yourselves, and checking arithmetic you have already done. Every such use must be listed in Section 6.3.

AI must not write, rewrite, translate, design or decide anything in your proposal, slides or pitch. This includes AI features built into Word, Google Docs, Grammarly and Canva. Please read R-05 in full: it lists specific tools and common situations, and not knowing a feature used AI is not accepted as a defence.

Can our teacher, parents or seniors help us?

They can support you the way a coach would: discuss your ideas, ask difficult questions, point out weak spots and suggest what to read. They cannot write, edit, calculate or design any part of your proposal or slides.

Describe any significant feedback you received in Section 6.3. Your faculty advisor countersigns the Originality & AI Declaration, confirming this to the best of their knowledge.

What counts toward the page limit?

The main body (Sections 1.0 to 6.1, including the optional additional section and any figures or tables inside them) counts: up to 15 pages for Junior and 25 for Senior. The cover page, contents page, references (6.2), AI disclosure (6.3), declaration and appendices do not.

There is no limit on the number of figures, but figures placed in the main body use up main-body pages. Appendices are optional and judges may not read them, so anything essential to your argument must be in the main body.

Can we update our proposal after uploading it?

Yes, as many times as you like until the deadline. Upload the new PDF on the portal; only your latest upload before the deadline is evaluated.

Nothing can be changed after the deadline. We strongly recommend uploading a complete version at least a day early, then replacing it if you improve it.

What happens in Round 2, and what if one of us can't attend?

Up to 10 shortlisted teams per division present at DPS R.K. Puram: a 6-minute (Junior) or 8-minute (Senior) pitch using up to 10 content slides (title and thank-you slides not counted), followed by approximately 10 minutes of questions, which may include a curveball card.

At least two team members must attend and present in person. Members who can't attend remain part of the team and share its result.

If our pitch goes badly, can we still win?

Yes. The final ranking is the combined total of your Round 1 score and your Round 2 score, out of 200. Your proposal score is carried forward in full, so a creative, rigorous proposal continues to count even if the live presentation doesn't go to plan.

Ties are broken by the Round 1 score first, which rewards strong written work.

What if we aren't shortlisted?

Every eligible proposal is scored by at least two judges on the same published rubric. Details of participation certificates and any feedback for teams that are not shortlisted will be published on the CelesteCon portal.

Where do we find deadlines and the finale date?

On the CelesteCon portal, published at registration, and by email to faculty advisors. All deadlines are in Indian Standard Time.

If an official update on the portal differs from this page, the later official update applies.

Who do we contact with a question?

Your faculty advisor should contact the organisers in writing, using the contact details on the CelesteCon portal. Ask questions about the rules, especially about AI use, before the Round 1 deadline. A written answer from the organisers is binding for that question.
