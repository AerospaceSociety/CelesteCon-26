In-PursuitOf DisputeThe 3v3 Chess-Clock Debate · Headline forensics of CelesteCon

Earn the floor with what you know. Keep it with how you argue. Lose it the second your clock falls.

In-Pursuit Of Dispute is the parliamentary forensics competition of CelesteCon 2026, open to school teams across India in Grades 6–12. It is built on one conviction: that serious argument about space, science and power needs two things at once, command of the facts and command of the room, and that most competitions only test one of them.

In Round 1, every debater sits a synchronised, optically proctored preliminary paper on Quizzitch, AEROSS's own assessment platform, and the three scores are pooled into one team score. The top sixteen teams nationwide are called to DPS R.K. Puram, New Delhi, for Round 2: a live tournament in which each team owns a single 15-minute bank on an electronic chess clock. Speak and your clock runs. Yield and theirs does. Run out while the contest is live, and you sit in silence while your opponents close the case.

Section 01 · The brief

Argument is combat, not recitation

Most school debating rewards the prepared speech: a polished eight minutes, delivered to a room that has agreed not to interrupt. The debates that matter do not work like that. A treaty negotiation, a launch review board, a parliamentary committee on orbital debris: each is a contest of people who know the facts, under a clock, answering the question they did not expect.

In-Pursuit Of Dispute is designed to reproduce that pressure honestly. Round 1 asks whether your team actually knows the science, law and history that space policy turns on. Round 2 asks whether you can use that knowledge while someone is trying to take it apart, and while your own time drains away every second you hold the floor.

True intellectual combat is the fusion of deep factual erudition with surgical, time-pressured dialectics. Knowledge without timing is a lecture. Timing without knowledge is noise.

What the chess clock changes

In a conventional format, time is fixed and fair: every speaker gets the same slot. Here, time is a resource your team spends. A long, careful constructive buys a strong foundation and costs you minutes you will want at the end. A sharp Point of Information costs up to fifteen of your own seconds and may cost your opponents a minute of recovery. Declining to speak saves time and surrenders the floor.

That single mechanic turns every decision in the round into a strategic one, and it makes the quality of an argument visible in a new way: the best teams say more in less time.

Why a quiz gateway

Debate topics about space are only as good as the facts under them. A motion on debris liability collapses if neither side knows the difference between absolute and fault-based liability. The Quizzitch preliminary makes that knowledge the price of entry, tests it under exam conditions that cannot be gamed, and uses the result to seed the tournament so that the strongest knowledge teams do not meet in their first debate.

Who this is for

Teams of three from the same school who read widely, think fast and like being disagreed with. You do not need prior debate experience. You do need to be willing to be interrupted, to concede a small point to win a large one, and to stop talking when the clock says so.

T1

The erudition & fact test

Quizzitch, Round 1. Each debater proves command of orbital mechanics, astrophysics, aerospace history, space law and policy, frontier technology, geopolitics and science ethics, alone, in a 45-minute synchronised window, under on-device optical proctoring and fullscreen lock.

Fails when: a team relies on one strong member; a candidate guesses carelessly into negative marking; anyone treats the integrity rules as optional.

T2

The clock management test

Ration a 15:00 bank across a constructive of 3:00 to 5:00, a blitz of turns between 0:30 and 3:00, every Point of Information you ask, and a closing you must declare before zero.

Fails when: a team flags with arguments unsaid; spends 6 minutes on turns that say nothing; or rests so early the opposition gets five uncontested minutes.

T3

The argumentative structure test

Definitions that are fair, a framework with a clear burden of proof, mechanisms that explain why something would happen, and impacts that are weighed, not just listed.

Fails when: the case is a list of assertions; the burden shifts mid-round; or the team never explains why its impacts outweigh the other side's.

T4

The cross-fire composure test

Take the Point of Information you did not want, answer it in one breath, and return to your line. Rebut an argument you have heard for the first time forty seconds ago, looking at the person who made it.

Fails when: a speaker declines every POI; answers a question with a speech; or loses the thread after an interjection.

01.1What wins in the chamber

I

Dialectical precision

Matter · 40% of speaker score

Arguments that are logically complete, directly engage the opposing case and weigh competing impacts. Rebuttal that attacks the strongest version of the other side, not a straw man.

Judge asks: if I remove this speaker's claims, which part of the case collapses?

II

Chamber presence

Manner · 30% of speaker score

Clarity, pace, emphasis and poise under interruption. Eye contact with the bench being answered. Persuasion that respects the audience's intelligence.

Judge asks: would a well-informed stranger in the gallery have been persuaded?

III

Evidential grounding

Method · 15% of speaker score

Structure that a listener can follow, consistency with teammates, and claims tied to real treaties, data, missions and precedents. Evidence named, not invented.

Judge asks: could I write down this speaker's structure after one hearing?

IV

Strategic time conservation

Clock & POI · 15% of speaker score

Floor turns sized to their content, POIs that cost the opponent more than they cost you, and a close planned before the bank runs low.

Judge asks: did this team spend its minutes where the debate was decided?

01.2Three debaters, three jobs

Speaker 1 · Prime Minister / Leader of Opposition

The architect

Delivers the protected constructive: definitions, framework, burden, metrics and the two or three substantive arguments the whole case rests on. Holds 3:00 to 5:00 of uninterrupted floor, then may only offer Points of Information for the rest of the debate.

Clock: 20–33% of the team's bank, spent in one block.

Speaker 2 · Deputy

The striker

Opens the blitz for the team, carries most of the direct rebuttal and the first reconstruction. Typically the team's most frequent Point of Information and its fastest thinker on the floor.

Clock: short turns, 0:30–1:30 each, several per debate.

Speaker 3 · Whip

The closer

Watches the bank, identifies the two or three clashes that decide the debate, and delivers the team's final turns and the declaration of rest. Owns the decision of when to stop.

Clock: protects a reserve of 1:30–2:30 for the close.

These roles are guidance, not rules. The only fixed requirements are that Speaker 1 delivers the constructive, and that Speakers 2 and 3 each hold the floor for at least 1:00 in total during Phase 2 (R-07.9).

01.3What this competition is, and is not

Not this

  • Memorised speeches read to a silent room.
  • Quizzing as a separate event with its own trophy and no consequence for the debate.
  • Winning by speaking longest, loudest or fastest.
  • Statistics nobody can source, quotations nobody said.
  • An internal or intra-school event.

This

  • Prepared foundations, then live, contested exchange under a clock.
  • An integrated gateway: prelim knowledge earns the floor and sets the bracket.
  • Winning by saying the decisive thing in the least time, and knowing when to stop.
  • Claims tied to named treaties, missions and data, open to an evidence challenge.
  • A nationwide competition for school teams from across India.

02.4Specimen prelim items

Four items in the style of the paper. They are not drawn from the live bank, which is never published.

Q-A · MCQ

A satellite in a circular orbit 400 km above Earth's surface completes one orbit in approximately:

  1. 64 minutes
  2. 92 minutes
  3. 128 minutes
  4. 24 hours

B · 92 minutes. From T = 2π√(a³/μ) with a ≈ 6,771 km and μ ≈ 398,600 km³/s², T ≈ 5,540 s ≈ 92 min, close to the International Space Station's period. 24 hours is geostationary altitude (about 35,786 km).

Q-C · MCQ

Under the 1972 Convention on International Liability for Damage Caused by Space Objects, a launching State's liability for damage its space object causes on the surface of the Earth is:

  1. Fault-based
  2. Absolute
  3. Limited to the insured value
  4. Shared equally by all UN members

B · Absolute (Article II). Damage caused elsewhere than on the surface, for example to another object in orbit, is fault-based (Article III). That asymmetry is at the heart of every debris motion.

Q-B · MCQ

India's first satellite, Aryabhata, reached orbit in 1975 aboard:

  1. SLV-3 from Sriharikota
  2. A Soviet Kosmos-3M from Kapustin Yar
  3. An American Scout from Wallops Island
  4. PSLV from Sriharikota

B. Aryabhata was launched on 19 April 1975 by a Soviet Kosmos-3M from Kapustin Yar. India's first indigenous orbital launch was SLV-3 with Rohini in 1980.

Q-F · Reasoning

"Since the 2019, 2021 and 2007 anti-satellite tests each created debris, any intercept in orbit will create long-lived debris." Which fact most weakens this inference?

  1. Debris lifetime depends strongly on the altitude of the intercept
  2. Some tests were condemned internationally
  3. Debris is tracked by several national networks
  4. Satellites can manoeuvre to avoid debris

A. The inference generalises from three cases without accounting for altitude, which governs how quickly atmospheric drag removes fragments. Options B–D may all be true but do not touch the claim about longevity.

03

Section 03 · Operational manual

The 3v3 chess-clock arena

This section is the authoritative description of how a Round 2 debate runs. It is restated formally as Rule R-07 in Section 05; where the two differ in wording, R-07 governs. Read it as a team, then train on the simulator in 03.7 until the timings are instinctive.

Terms used throughout: PROPthe Proposition (Speakers P1, P2, P3; P1 is the Prime Minister) and OPPthe Opposition (O1, O2, O3; O1 is the Leader of the Opposition). A floor turn is one continuous period in which one debater holds the floor. A strike is pressing your side's clock button, which stops your clock and starts theirs.

03.1The bank, the flag and the rest

15:00

One shared bank per team

Each team's three debaters draw on a single 15-minute clock. It runs only while one of them holds the floor or asks an accepted Point of Information. It never runs while the other team speaks. Time cannot be borrowed, gifted, banked across debates or added back, except by the Clock Marshal correcting an error.

00:00

Flag fall

If a team's clock reaches zero while the other team is still live, that team has flagged. It loses all speaking rights at once, mid-sentence if need be, may not offer or accept POIs, and forfeits the ballot. The other team may use its remaining time to close, uncontested.

REST

The declaration of rest

A team ends its part in the debate by declaring, while holding the floor, "The Proposition rests" (or Opposition) and striking the clock. Its remaining time is forfeited. Once one team rests, the other may close; if its clock then runs out, that is a closing expiry, not a flag.

03.2The two phases

Phase 1 · The constructives

1st speakers · protected foundation
3:00–5:00

The Chair opens the debate and starts the Proposition clock. P1 delivers the constructive: definitions, framework, burden of proof, metrics for judging, and the primary substantive arguments. P1 strikes the clock; O1 then delivers the Opposition constructive, which may contest the definition only if it is unreasonable (R-08.6), sets the Opposition framework and arguments, and may begin rebuttal.

  • Uninterrupted. No Points of Information may be offered during either constructive.
  • No passing. The clock cannot be struck during a constructive before 3:00, and cannot be passed to a teammate at all. If a first speaker stops before 3:00, the clock keeps running to 3:00 in silence.
  • Ceiling. At 5:00 the Chair raps once. At 5:15 the Chair calls order and the Marshal strikes the clock (R-07.4).
  • Consecutive. O1 must begin within 10 seconds of P1's strike. The Opposition clock runs from the strike.

When O1 strikes, Phase 2 begins and the floor passes to the Proposition.

Phase 2 · The blitz

2nd & 3rd speakers · dynamic cross-fire
0:30–3:00

The floor now moves as the teams choose. Only Speakers 2 and 3 of each team may hold the floor in Phase 2. First speakers remain in the chamber and may offer Points of Information, but may not hold the floor again.

  • Minimum floor: 0:30. Every floor turn lasts at least thirty seconds before the holder may yield, pass to a teammate or rest. This prevents hot-potato clock toggling.
  • Maximum continuous speech: 3:00. No debater may hold the floor for more than three minutes without yielding to the opponents or passing to a teammate. Warning at 2:30; Chair's rap at 3:00; order and forced strike at 3:15.
  • Yield. "I yield the floor to the Opposition", then strike. The opponents' chosen speaker rises at once.
  • Teammate pass. "I pass to my colleague." The clock is not struck; the team's clock keeps running; the new speaker's turn timer starts at zero.
  • Points of Information may be offered once a turn has passed 0:30 (03.4).

03.4Point of Information protocol

  1. Offer

    A debater on the side without the floor stands and says "Point of Information" or "On that point". Offers are not permitted in Phase 1, in the first 0:30 of a turn, or by a team that has rested or flagged.

    Clock: unchanged. Holder's clock keeps running.

  2. Accept or decline

    The holder says "Accepted" or "No, thank you" and may finish the sentence first. Declining is legitimate; declining every POI across a debate is marked down under Clock & POI.

    Clock: unchanged if declined. The same team may not re-offer within 0:15 of a decline.

  3. Strike to the asker

    On accepting, the holder strikes the clock. The asker's team clock now runs, and the holder's turn timer pauses.

    Clock: asker's bank runs.

  4. Ask in ≤ 0:15

    The asker puts one crisp question or statement. No preamble, no second question, no follow-up. At 0:15 the Chair says "Time".

    Clock: hard cap 0:15; overrun charged and marked down.

  5. Strike back & sit

    The asker strikes the clock back and sits. If the asker forgets, the Marshal strikes at 0:15. The holder's clock resumes and their turn timer continues from where it paused.

    Clock: holder's bank runs again.

  6. Answer and move on

    The holder answers on their own time. A good answer is one sentence and a return to the line; a bad one is a new speech. The Chair does not intervene in the quality of answers.

    Clock: answer time counts toward the holder's 3:00 cap.

If the asker's bank reaches zero during an accepted POI while the holder's team is still live, the asker's team has flagged (R-07.7). Ask short.

03.5Flag fall, rest and the close

Flag fall · while the contest is live

A contest is live while neither team has rested or flagged. If either clock reaches 00:00 while the contest is live, the Marshal calls "Flag". That team's time has expired completely:

  • Its speaker stops immediately. Words after the flag are disregarded by the panel.
  • It may not speak, offer or accept POIs, or rebut for the rest of the debate.
  • The other team takes the floor with its choice of Speaker 2 or 3 and may use its remaining bank to summarise, subject to the normal 3:00 cap and teammate passes.
  • The ballot is awarded to the other team by every adjudicator. Speaker scores are still given for everything said before the flag; the flagged debater's Clock & POI score is capped at 9.

Rest and closing expiry

Resting is how a team finishes on its own terms. After a rest:

  • The resting team's clock stops for good and its remaining time is forfeited.
  • The other team holds the floor and may close. The resting team may not offer POIs.
  • If the closing team's clock then reaches zero, that is a closing expiry: the debate ends with no forfeit. Words after zero are disregarded.
  • If the closing team rests too, the debate ends and the ballots are decided on the merits.

New matter in the close. A team that holds the floor after its opponent has rested may not introduce a new substantive argument; it may extend, rebut and weigh. Panels disregard new substantive matter introduced once the opponent can no longer answer.

03.6Officers of the chamber

Presides

The Chair

Opens and closes the debate, calls speakers, raps at caps, calls "Time" on POIs, rules on points of order and equity. Can hold both clocks for a procedural stop. Usually also the chair of the adjudication panel.

Keeps time

The Clock Marshal

Sits at the clock with a turn timer and a POI timer. Shows amber at 2:30, strikes on forced yields and POI overruns, calls the flag, and keeps the written clock log that settles any dispute (R-07.10).

Decides

The adjudication panel

Three adjudicators (five in the Grand Final). Each writes an independent ballot with six speaker scores, a POI-impact tally and a decision, before a short consultation and the oral adjudication.

Records

The tab room

Collects ballots and clock logs, computes standings and the break, publishes the speaker tab, and investigates evidence challenges within 15 minutes of the debate ending.

      Tier 1
      Legitimate research

      Any time before the prep room · no declaration

      Prohibited

        Phase 2 turns. Only Speakers 2 and 3 hold the floor. Every turn lasts at least 0:30 before its holder may yield, pass to a teammate or rest.
      4. 7.4
        Caps. No debater speaks continuously for more than 3:00 in Phase 2 (5:00 in Phase 1). The Chair raps at the cap. At cap + 0:15 the Chair calls order and the Marshal strikes the clock to the opponents, or passes to the holder's teammate if the opponents have rested or flagged. One point is deducted from the holder's Clock & POI score per breach.
      5. 7.5
        Points of Information. Offered only in Phase 2, after the first 0:30 of a turn, by a live team. On acceptance the holder strikes; the asker speaks for no more than 0:15 on its own team's clock, strikes back and sits. The holder's turn timer pauses during the POI. The Marshal returns the clock at 0:15.
      6. 7.6
        Teammate pass. After 0:30, the holder may pass to their Phase 2 teammate without striking. The team clock continues; the new turn timer starts at zero.
      7. 7.7
        Flag fall. A contest is live while neither team has rested or flagged. A team whose clock reaches 00:00 while the contest is live has flagged: it loses all speaking rights immediately, cannot offer or accept POIs, and forfeits the ballot of every adjudicator. The other team may use its remaining time to close. Speaker scores for the flagged team are awarded for what was said before the flag, with Clock & POI capped at 9 for the debater holding the floor.
      8. 7.8
        Rest & closing expiry. In Phase 2, after 0:30 of a turn, the holder may declare the team's rest and strike. The resting team's remaining time is forfeited. If the other team's clock then reaches 00:00, the debate ends without forfeit (closing expiry). A team closing after its opponent has rested may not introduce new substantive matter.
      9. 7.9
        Participation. Speakers 2 and 3 of each team must each hold at least 1:00 of floor in total in Phase 2, unless their team flags or the opponents flag first. Otherwise −2 Method to that debater.
      10. 7.10
        Officials. The Chair presides and may hold both clocks for a procedural stop. The Clock Marshal keeps a written log of every strike, POI and ruling; that log settles any dispute about time.
      11. 7.11
        Malfunction. If a clock fails, the Chair holds the debate, the Marshal restores both banks from the log and a backup clock, and the debate resumes with the same holder.
      R-08

      Motions, preparation & the Chamber Lock

      1. 8.1
        The Motion Pool of twelve motions, two in each domain D-I to D-VI, with Info Slides, is released to finalist advisors exactly 48 hours before the opening bell.
      2. 8.2
        The SHA-256 digest of the Motion Pool is published with the national shortlist. The released pool must reproduce that digest; any team may verify it.
      3. 8.3
        Each Chamber Round motion is drawn by lot, in public, from the motions not yet used, 30 minutes before the round. Elimination motions are sealed, are not in the pool, and are opened 30 minutes before each round.
      4. 8.4
        Each team may bring one evidence binder of at most 60 printed A4 sides of sourced material (extracts, data, quotations with citations). A binder may not contain scripted speeches; reading a pre-written speech verbatim in the chamber is marked down under Manner.
      5. 8.5
        Prep lasts 30 minutes in the allocated room, three debaters only, with the permitted materials listed in 04.2. No advisor or outside contact.
      6. 8.6
        Definitions must be reasonable and debatable. The Opposition may challenge a definition only in O1's constructive, and only if it is unreasonable, truistic or squirrelled; the panel then decides which definition the debate is judged on.
      7. 8.7
        The released dossier is shared with finalists for preparation and may not be posted publicly until the tournament ends.
      R-09

      Adjudication, ballots & tabulation

      1. 9.1
        Panels are three adjudicators in Chamber Rounds, quarterfinals and semifinals, and five in the Grand Final, drawn from experienced debate adjudicators, faculty, and specialists in space science and policy.
      2. 9.2
        No adjudicator judges a team from a school with which they have a current or recent connection. Conflicts are declared before the draw.
      3. 9.3
        Each adjudicator completes an independent ballot: six speaker scores on the 60–80 scale, a POI-impact tally and a decision, submitted before any conferral.
      4. 9.4
        The winning team on each ballot must have the higher team total, except where the result is decided by flag fall. Equal totals are not permitted.
      5. 9.5
        After ballots are submitted the Chair delivers an oral adjudication of up to 8 minutes explaining the decision and the key clashes.
      6. 9.6
        Standings, the break and the speaker tab are computed as in 02.5 and published after Round III. Full ballots are released to advisors after the tournament.
      R-10

      Conduct, equity & safeguarding

      1. 10.1
        Attack arguments, never people. Personal remarks about an opponent, and remarks that demean anyone for gender, religion, caste, region, language, disability or background, breach the equity policy.
      2. 10.2
        An equity officer is present throughout Round 2. Any debater, advisor or adjudicator may raise a concern privately. The Chair may pause a debate to address a breach.
      3. 10.3
        Sanctions range from a warning to a Manner deduction, loss of the round and disqualification, depending on severity and repetition.
      4. 10.4
        Most participants are minors, and all are under the care of their school. Adults on campus follow DPS R.K. Puram's safeguarding procedures; debaters are never alone with an adult official outside a chamber or prep room.
      5. 10.5
        Tampering with clocks, ballots or the tab, and deliberate fabrication of evidence, are serious misconduct.
      R-11

      1. 11.1
        A dispute about time is raised with the Marshal immediately, in the round. It is settled from the Marshal's log and cannot be raised afterwards.
      2. 11.2

      3. 11.3

      4. 11.4

      5. 11.5

      06

      Section 06 · Adjudication rubrics & speaker FAQ

      How speeches are scored, and what debaters ask

      Every adjudicator scores every debater on the same 60–80 scale, built from four criteria. The scale is deliberately narrow: most speeches in a strong national field land between 66 and 76, and a single point is a real difference. Adjudicators score the speech they heard, not the grade, school or reputation of the speaker.

      06.1The speaker scale

      60–64

      Developing

      Hard to follow; claims without reasons; little engagement; clock managed poorly or not at all.

      65–69

      Emerging

      Clear in parts; some reasoning; rebuttal present but thin; turns too long or too short.

      70

      Competent

      The average speech at this tournament. Relevant, structured, engages the other side, keeps to time.

      71–75

      Strong

      Well-reasoned and well-weighed; POIs handled cleanly; turns sized to their content.

      76–80

      Exceptional

      Changes the debate. Decisive clash identified and won; flawless poise; clock used as a weapon. 80 is rare.

      06.3Debater & quizzing FAQ

      Round 1 · Quizzitch

      Q01

      What do we need technically to sit the Quizzitch paper?

      For each of the three debaters: a laptop or desktop with a working webcam, a current version of Chrome or Edge, a stable broadband connection, and a quiet room where they are alone. The camera should face you at eye level with light in front of you, not behind. Close every other application and disable browser extensions before you start.

      Run the readiness check in 02.1 on the same device and browser a few days before the window, and again on the day.

      Q02

      Do the three of us sit the paper together?

      No. Each debater sits alone on their own device, at the same time, and may not communicate with teammates during the window. Two people in one frame is a Level 2 strike; working together is a Level 3 breach. Your team score is simply the sum of three independent papers.

      Q03

      What if my internet drops or my laptop crashes mid-exam?

      Rejoin immediately with the same Team ID and your own name. The window does not pause, so every minute counts. Note the time, and afterwards tell your advisor, who reports it by email within 30 minutes of the window closing (R-03.6). The gap appears in your integrity ledger as a connection event; a short, reported gap is a Level 1 advisory with no deduction.

      Individual re-sits are not offered. If many candidates are affected by a platform failure, R-12.3 applies.

      Q04

      Is my video recorded? What happens to my data?

      The webcam feed is analysed on your own device by the detection model; as configured for CelesteCon 2026 it is not streamed or stored as video. The platform keeps your baseline photo and a timestamped event ledger for the integrity audit. Your advisor confirms parental or guardian consent to this at registration.

      Q05

      Can I wear glasses, a head covering or a hearing aid?

      Yes to glasses and religious or cultural head coverings, provided your face is visible for the presence check. Medically required hearing aids are permitted if declared by the advisor in advance. Headphones and earbuds of any kind are not permitted.

      Q06

      Can I use a calculator or rough paper?

      Two blank sheets and a pen, shown to the camera at calibration. No calculator: items are written to be solved by estimation and reasoning, and any numerical item states its tolerance. A calculator may be detected as a device.

      Q07

      Should we guess on items we don't know?

      On single-correct and source-based items, a wrong answer costs 1 mark and a right one earns 5, so eliminating two options makes a guess worthwhile. Multi-select and numerical items carry no negative marking.

      Round 2 · The chess-clock arena

      Q08

      What happens if our team flags out in the middle of a speech?

      If the contest is still live (neither side has rested), the Marshal calls "Flag" and your speaker stops mid-sentence. Your team can no longer speak, offer POIs or answer anything. The opponents take the floor and may use whatever remains on their clock to summarise, and every adjudicator awards them the ballot (R-07.7).

      You still receive speaker scores for everything said before the flag, so a strong speech is not wasted on the tab. The debater holding the floor at the flag is capped at 9 for Clock & POI. The lesson: watch the bank, and rest before it runs out.

      Q09

      If the other team rests, do we have to use all our remaining time?

      No. You may close and then rest whenever you choose. If you keep speaking until your clock reaches zero, that is a closing expiry with no penalty; the debate simply ends. You may not introduce new substantive arguments once the opponents have rested, because they can no longer answer.

      Q10

      Can our first speaker speak again after the constructive?

      Not on the floor. After Phase 1, only Speakers 2 and 3 hold the floor. Speaker 1 remains active by offering Points of Information, which is often where an architect of the case does the most damage.

      Q11

      What if a speaker hits the clock before 30 seconds?

      The strike is void. The Marshal restores the clocks and the speaker continues until at least 0:30. A first early strike carries no penalty; a repeat in the same debate costs a point of Clock & POI. In a constructive, the clock simply runs to 3:00 whatever the speaker does.

      Q12

      Who pays for a Point of Information, and does it count against my 3:00?

      The asking team pays for the question, up to 0:15, on its own clock. Your turn timer pauses while the question is asked, so the question does not count toward your 3:00. Your answer does, and is on your clock.

      Q13

      Can we bring laptops, phones or printed material into the prep room?

      No electronic devices of any kind: phones, laptops, tablets, smartwatches and earpieces are deposited at accreditation. You may bring your printed evidence binder (up to 60 A4 sides of sourced material, no scripted speeches), blank paper, pens, a printed dictionary and a non-networked stopwatch (R-08.4, R-08.5).

      Q14

      Can our advisor coach us during preparation?

      Yes, throughout the 48-hour window before the tournament, and between rounds. No, in the prep room or during a debate. Advisors sit in the gallery and may not signal to the team.

      Q15

      How are ties on the speaker tab broken?

      For the Speaker of the Conclave: the highest single-round score, then the higher Matter total across Chamber Rounds, then the higher individual Quizzitch score. For the team standings: wins, ballots, team speaker points, then prelim seed (02.5).

      Q16

      How are sides decided?

      By lot in Round I. In Rounds II and III sides are balanced so that no team is on the same side in all three. In elimination rounds, the higher break seed's side is decided by lot in public at the motion release.

      Q17

      What is an evidence challenge?

      After a debate, a team that believes an opponent used a fabricated fact may ask the tab room, within 15 minutes, to check one specific claim. The claiming team names its source. Unsupported claims can cost up to 4 Matter points; deliberate fabrication is referred as misconduct. It never changes who won the debate (R-05.8).

      Logistics

      Q18

      Is this open to schools outside Delhi?

      In-Pursuit Of Dispute is a nationwide competition open to recognised schools across India. Round 1 is online so that distance is no barrier to qualifying.

      Q19

      Do Grade 6 debaters really compete against Grade 12?

      Yes, in one open division. The motions are written to be accessible to a well-read twelve-year-old and demanding for a seventeen-year-old. Younger debaters are also recognised through the Rising Orator award, and many of the strongest teams mix grades.

      Q20

      What should we bring to Round 2?

      School identity cards, the signed integrity declaration, your evidence binder, pens and paper, water, and school uniform or formal attire. Leave devices with your advisor or at the deposit desk. The binding run-sheet in the released dossier tells you when and where to report.
