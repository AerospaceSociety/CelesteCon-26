Section 01 · The brief

Lift is calculated.
Fit is modelled.

An aircraft that works on paper fails the day its battery will not fit behind the wing. A beautiful render is worthless if its lift-to-drag ratio cannot hold level flight. Volatus rewards the team that can prove both.

What Volatus is

Volatus is the aircraft design competition of CelesteCon, the aerospace and STEM conclave of AEROSS at Delhi Public School, R.K. Puram. Each division receives one formal Request for Proposal (RFP) from a fictional operator with a real mission, a real place, a real atmosphere and a fixed set of limits. Teams respond with a Technical Dossier that designs one complete uncrewed aircraft for that mission: its wing and airfoil, its propulsion and batteries, its structure and materials, its avionics and sensors, its operations and its risks, and a complete 3D CAD model that proves all of it physically fits together.

Why Dimension III now lives inside Volatus

In real aerospace programmes, 3D design is not a showreel. At every serious design house, the CAD model is the aircraft before the aircraft exists: it holds the loft that the aerodynamicist analyses, the mass properties that decide where the centre of gravity falls, the section cuts that show whether the avionics stack clears the spar, and the drawings that a workshop will one day cut metal and lay up carbon from. So from Edition VI, Dimension III is no longer a separate rendering and animation contest. It is the digital prototyping half of Volatus, scored on the same aircraft and by the same jury.

How the design actually converges

Aircraft design is a spiral, not a line. You guess a mass, size a wing for it, choose a motor for the drag of that wing, pick a battery for the energy that motor needs, then model it all and discover the battery is 400 g heavier and 60 mm longer than the bay. So the mass goes up, the wing grows, the drag rises, and you go round again. The teams that win are the ones who show this loop honestly, with each pass recorded in their calculations ledger and each change visible in their CAD history, until the numbers and the model agree.

T1Aerodynamics

The Aerodynamics & Polar Test

Does it fly, by the numbers? Lift, drag, airfoil choice and stall margin derived from real fluid dynamics at your mission's air density and Reynolds number.

  • Airfoil chosen from real section data at the right Reynolds number
  • Lift equals weight at cruise with a sensible CL
  • Stall speed inside the RFP limit, with margin

Fails when: CL of 2.5 on a plain wing; drag ignored; sea-level density used at 5,600 m.

T2CAD packaging

The CAD Packaging & Volumetric Test

Does it fit, in 3D? Every component modelled at true size, placed with clearances, wired, and balanced so the centre of gravity sits ahead of the neutral point.

  • Section cut showing battery, avionics, payload and harness with clearances
  • Mass properties from the model agree with the mass budget
  • CG inside the stable window, loaded and empty

Fails when: an empty fuselage shell; components floating in space; a CG that moves behind the NP when the payload drops.

T3Power

The Power & Propulsion Test

Does it get there and back? Thrust-to-weight, motor–propeller matching, battery energy and the mission energy budget with a real reserve.

  • Motor, ESC and propeller matched at the operating point
  • Segment-by-segment energy budget in Wh
  • Usable battery energy, not nameplate energy

Fails when: 100% depth of discharge; propulsive efficiency of 1.0; endurance quoted from a hobby shop listing.

T4Defence

The Cross-Examination Test

Can you defend it, live? Structural sizing, fastener choices, manufacturing tolerances and every number in the ledger, under direct questioning from people who design aircraft for a living.

  • Any member re-derives any key figure on a whiteboard
  • The team sections and measures its own model on request
  • Honest answers about what is not yet known

Fails when: "our software calculated it"; a drawing no one on the team can read.

WHAT CHANGES IN 2026From rendered to engineered

Retired: 3D as decoration

  • Photorealistic renders and animated advertisements judged on looks
  • Style words (modern, elegant, intimidating) in place of requirements
  • Vehicles with "many sensors" and no stated mass, volume or power
  • Proving something is possible by showing a picture of it
  • Brand-name products and invented model numbers presented as real

Required: 3D as engineering

  • A parametric, fully constrained CAD assembly of the whole aircraft
  • Dimensioned 3-view drawings with CG and neutral point marked
  • Exploded assemblies naming real ribs, spars, fasteners, servos and mounts
  • Section cuts proving every component fits, with measured clearances
  • A loft built from real airfoil coordinates, analysed for lift and drag

Renderings are still welcome, as long as they are clearly labelled as illustrations and the engineering figures in the CAD register are present. A render never substitutes for a drawing.

WHAT WINSThe four judging pillars

I

Aerodynamic rigour

Choices made from section data, polars and trade studies at the right density and Reynolds number, not from how a wing looks.

Juror asks: "Show me where your cruise CL sits on your airfoil's polar."

II

Mechanical CAD integration

A model that is the aircraft: constrained, dimensioned, massed, sectioned and drawn to a standard a workshop could build from.

Juror asks: "Cut the fuselage at the wing spar. What is the clearance to the battery?"

III

Defensible mathematics

Every figure traceable to a formula, an input and a source in the calculations ledger, and consistent everywhere it appears.

Juror asks: "Your energy budget says 83 Wh. Your summary says 78. Which one flies?"

IV

Operational resilience

An aircraft designed for its worst day: wind, heat, lost links, failed actuators, and a safe outcome for people on the ground.

Juror asks: "GPS drops out over the river. What does the aircraft do in the next ten seconds?"

Section 02 · Format & scoring

Two rounds. Both count.

Volatus runs as a design review cycle, the way a real aircraft programme moves from a paper proposal to a critical design review. The dossier is read and scored first, without the reviewers knowing who wrote it. The shortlisted teams then stand in front of the aircraft they designed and defend it in person.

  1. 1

    Register

    Form a team of 2–4 students from one school, choose your division and name a faculty advisor on the CelesteCon portal.

    Portal · IST
  2. 2

    Round 1 · Dossier & CAD

    Answer your division's RFP with the dossier architecture in Section 04, including the full CAD package, and upload one PDF before the deadline.

    Asynchronous · PDF
  3. 3

    Double-blind evaluation

    At least two judges score every eligible dossier on the Round 1 rubric. Originality, AI-use, CAD and calculation audits run on all entries.

    Anonymised reading
  4. 4

    Shortlist

    Up to 10 teams per division advance. Finalists receive written judge comments to prepare their defence.

    Portal & advisor email
  5. 5

    Round 2 · Flight review

    Live pitch, CAD interrogation, cross-examination and curveball at DPS R.K. Puram, New Delhi. Results at the closing ceremony.

    On campus · live

Double-blind means judges do not see team names, school names or student names while scoring Round 1, and teams do not know which judges read their dossier. For this reason the cover page is removed before judges receive the PDF, and names, school logos and school names must not appear anywhere else in Sections 1.0 to 7.0, including inside CAD title blocks (use your team's registration code instead; see Section 04).

R1 + R2 = 200Final score · combined total

Your dossier score carries all the way to the final result

Finalists are ranked on the combined total of their Round 1 dossier score (out of 100) and their Round 2 flight review score (out of 100). Round 1 does not reset at the finale. Exhaustive aerodynamic analysis, careful CAD work and an honest calculations ledger keep their full weight in the final tally, so a team whose live presentation meets turbulence (a nervous start, a projector fault, a curveball that lands badly) remains fully in contention on the strength of its engineering.

If two teams finish level, the higher Round 1 score ranks first. If they are still level, the higher score on the Round 1 Calculations Ledger Integrity criterion decides. If they are still level, the higher score on the Round 1 Mechanical CAD Modelling & Drafting criterion decides.

Section 03 · The division challenges

Pick your division. Read your RFP.

Each division answers one Request for Proposal with one shared fact sheet, so every team starts from the same air, the same limits and the same mission, and the jury compares like with like. Read the whole RFP before you open your CAD software: the scenario, your role, the requirements, the fact sheet, the mandatory calculations and the sample curveballs are all part of the brief. The rubrics that score it are in Section 06.

Every organisation, operator, corridor and schedule in these RFPs is fictional. The geography, the atmosphere and the physics are real.

Junior Division · Grades 6–8 · Assam · Brahmaputra · Monsoon season

Floodline

When the river swallows the roads, send something that flies. Two medical pods, 24.5 kilometres, one charge, no runway.

VLT-JRFP · Brahmaputra Flood
Relief Authority

Majuli sits in the middle of the Brahmaputra in upper Assam: one of the largest river islands in the world, home to more than a lakh and a half people, dozens of villages and the historic satras of Assamese culture. For most of the year, ferries cross from Nimati Ghat on the Jorhat side in about an hour. But every monsoon, from June to September, the Brahmaputra rises. Channels merge into kilometres of fast brown water, the ferries stop when the river is in spate, and roads on the island disappear under flood.

When that happens, the people who most need help are the hardest to reach: a child with a fever in a village cut off by water, a health sub-centre that has run out of oral rehydration salts, a family waiting on a rooftop. Boats are slow and dangerous in the current. Helicopters are few, expensive and grounded in heavy rain.

The Brahmaputra Flood Relief Authority (BFRA), a fictional state relief agency, has decided to build a fleet of small, autonomous, battery-electric aircraft that can carry medical supplies across the river to marked drop zones on Majuli and fly home again, in monsoon wind and rain, many times a day. It has been granted a (fictional) relief flight corridor across the river, and it has written the mission limits on the fact sheet below. Your team is one of the design groups invited to respond to RFP VLT-J.

The challenge

Design Floodline, an uncrewed fixed-wing aircraft that launches from a bungee rail on the embankment at Nimati Ghat, flies autonomously across the river, drops two 0.5 kg medical pods on two separate drop zones on Majuli, flies home and lands, all on one battery charge. Choose its layout, design its wing, size its motor and battery, design the mechanism that releases each pod on command, and build the whole aircraft as a 3D CAD model in which every part really fits. Then prove, with numbers and with drawings, that it flies.

The ground rules

  • Use the Floodline Fact Sheet below for every value it covers. If you need a number that is not on it, use a real, cited figure (for example from a motor or battery datasheet) or a clearly explained assumption, and record it in Section 7.1.
  • Use only parts and materials that exist today and that a school workshop or a small drone company could buy or make. If you choose a real motor, propeller or battery, cite its datasheet.
  • The aircraft is electric. No fuel engines, no rockets, no balloons.
  • Everything in the dossier is designed on paper and in CAD. You are not required to build or fly the aircraft, and a flying prototype earns no extra points.
  • No technology that breaks the laws of physics, and no "future batteries" with energy densities no one can buy.

What a great entry looks like

A great Junior dossier reads like a design a real drone workshop could start building on Monday: a layout chosen for good reasons, one airfoil chosen from real data, a wing sized so it cannot stall on launch, a battery that brings the aircraft home with energy to spare, a pod release that a judge can see working in the drawings, a CAD model with every part inside it, and ten calculations a judge can check line by line.

What we will look for first

  • Your 3-view drawing (CD-1) with span, chord, length and the CG mark
  • Your stall speed (J-C4) against the 11.0 m/s limit
  • Your energy budget (J-C7) against your battery
  • Your packaging section (CD-3) with both pods in place
  • Your CG, with and without pods (J-C8)

Words to know before you start

  • MTOW: maximum take-off weight, everything included
  • Airfoil: the cross-section shape of the wing
  • Chord: the width of the wing from front edge to back edge
  • Stall: when the wing stops making enough lift
  • CG: centre of gravity, the balance point
  • MAC: mean aerodynamic chord, the "average" chord

Conventional tractor

A fuselage with a wing in the middle, a tail at the back and the propeller at the nose. The classic trainer layout. Pods sit in a bay under the wing, close to the CG.

No tail: the whole aircraft is one swept wing with a thick centre body. The lowest drag and the most compact in a transport case, but the least forgiving.

MANDATORY CALCULATIONS10 figures · J-C1 to J-C10

Each calculation must appear in the section shown, with the formula, your numbers with units, the result, and a reference to its line in the Section 7.2 calculations ledger. Every number you use must come from the fact sheet, your CAD model, a cited source or a stated assumption in 7.1.

J-C1→ 2.2

Wing area & mean chord

How big is your wing? Every other number depends on this one.

S = b × (cr + ct) / 2 · c̄ = (2/3) cr (1 + λ + λ²) / (1 + λ), λ = ct/cr

Take span b, root chord cr and tip chord ct straight from your 3-view (CD-1). For a rectangular wing, S = b × c and c̄ = c. Give S in m².

J-C2→ 2.1

Aspect ratio & Reynolds number

Is the wing long and slender or short and stubby, and which airfoil data applies to it?

AR = b² / S · Re = ρ V c̄ / μ

Use V = 18 m/s. Your airfoil data must be for a Reynolds number close to yours; say which source and which Re you used.

Check: Floodline-sized wings sit roughly between 2 × 10⁵ and 4 × 10⁵.

J-C3→ 2.3

Dynamic pressure & cruise lift coefficient

In level flight, lift equals weight. How hard must the wing work to hold Floodline up?

q = ½ ρ V² · L = W = m g · CL = W / (q S)

Use MTOW. Mark your cruise CL on your airfoil's lift curve.

Check: a healthy cruise CL is well below CL,max, typically 0.3 to 0.7.

J-C4→ 2.3

Wing loading & stall speed

How slowly can Floodline fly before the wing gives up, and is the launch fast enough?

W/S (N/m²) · Vstall = √( 2W / (ρ S CL,max) )

CL,max = 0.9 × your airfoil's cl,max. Then check launch speed ÷ Vstall ≥ 1.25.

Must meet: Vstall ≤ 11.0 m/s at 5.0 kg (or your MTOW).

J-C5→ 2.4

Cruise drag & lift-to-drag ratio

Every newton of drag must be paid for with battery energy.

CD = CD,0 + CL² / (π e AR) · D = q S CD · L/D = CL / CD

Use CD,0 = 0.030 (or 0.036 with external pods) and e = 0.80. Show both parts of the drag: the part that doesn't depend on lift and the induced part.

J-C6→ 3.1

Thrust-to-weight & cruise power

Can the motor pull Floodline up the climb, and how much power does cruising cost?

T/W = Tstatic / W · Pelec = D V / η + Pavionics

Tstatic comes from the thrust table for your exact motor, propeller and battery voltage; cite it. η = 0.55; Pavionics = 7 W.

Must meet: T/W ≥ 0.50.

J-C7→ 3.3

Mission energy, battery & peak current

Does Floodline get home, with energy left over, and can the battery deliver the power the climb asks for?

E = Σ Pi ti (S1…S8) · Eusable = 0.8 × Vnom × C · reserve = (Eusable − E) / Eusable · Ipeak = Pclimb / Vnom

VLT-SRFP · Ladakh Aerial
Health Network

Ladakh is one of the highest inhabited regions on Earth. Leh, its capital, sits at about 3,500 metres; many of its villages sit higher. Health centres are scattered across valleys separated by passes above 5,000 metres, and the road from Leh to the Pangong and Changthang side crosses Chang La at about 5,360 metres. In summer the road is busy and slow; landslides and snow can close it for hours or days. A unit of blood, a dose of rabies immunoglobulin or a batch of vaccines that must stay between 2 °C and 8 °C can take most of a day to reach Tangtse by road, or not arrive at all.

Aircraft do not like Ladakh either. At Leh on a summer afternoon the air is so thin and warm that it behaves like air at more than 4,300 metres. Over Chang La it behaves like air at more than 6,000 metres. A rotor must push far more air, and a wing must fly far faster, to hold up the same weight. Batteries start cold at dawn and run hot in hover. Radios lose line of sight behind the ridge. Winds accelerate over the pass.

The Ladakh Aerial Health Network (LAHN), a fictional public health logistics service, wants a fleet of uncrewed vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) aircraft that take off from a pad in Leh, fly wing-borne over Chang La, and land on a small pad at the health centre in Tangtse, carrying a 4 kg insulated cold-chain box. It has a (fictional) approved flight corridor and a relay mast on the pass. Your team is one of the design consortia invited to respond to RFP VLT-S.

The challenge

Design the Thin Air aircraft: an electric eVTOL that meets every hard requirement below on the hot-day design atmosphere. Choose its architecture, select and analyse its airfoil at its real Reynolds number, build its drag polar from the wetted areas of your own CAD model, size its rotors, motors and battery for hover at altitude, prove its static margin in every loading state, size its wing spar and root joint for ultimate load, and deliver a fully constrained parametric 3D assembly with drawings a composite workshop could build from. The ground rules

  • Use the Thin Air Fact Sheet and the design atmosphere for every value they cover. Any other value must be real and cited (datasheet, textbook, NASA or peer-reviewed report), or a stated, justified assumption in Section 7.1.

  • All-electric propulsion from batteries. No fuel cells, engines, hybrid generators, tethers or solar panels as a power source for this leg.

CHOOSE YOUR ARCHITECTUREHow will Thin Air get from hover to wing?

Pick one primary architecture. Your Section 1.3 trade study must score all three against weighted criteria that you define and justify, with a sensitivity check showing whether the winner changes if the weights change.

A

Tilt-wing

The whole wing, with its motors, pivots from horizontal to vertical. In hover the wing is edge-on to the rotor wash; in transition the wash blows over the wing and delays stall.

FORLittle hover download; clean cruise; prop wash helps the wing through transition.AGAINSTHeavy pivot and large actuator loads; gusts at partial tilt; complex wing-root structure.
B

Tilt-rotor

The wing stays fixed; only the front motors or nacelles tilt. Rear lift rotors stop and align with the flow in cruise. A lighter mechanism than tilting the whole wing.

FORSmall, light tilt mechanism; fixed wing is simpler to build and stiffen.AGAINSTWing sits in the rotor wash in hover (about 10% download); stopped rear rotors add drag.
C

Lift + cruise

Four dedicated lift rotors on booms for hover, one pusher propeller for cruise, nothing that tilts. The most common layout among today's cargo VTOL drones.

FORNo tilt mechanism; simplest transition and control; each propeller optimised for one job.AGAINSTLift system is dead mass and drag in cruise; booms need torsional stiffness.

MANDATORY CALCULATIONS15 figures · S-C1 to S-C15

Each calculation must appear in the section shown, with the governing equation, inputs with units and sources, the result to about three significant figures, and a reference to its Section 7.2 ledger line. Where an input comes from your CAD model (a wetted area, a mass, a CG, a moment of inertia), show a screenshot of the measurement or mass-properties dialog in 7.2.

S-C1→ 1.2

Design atmosphere & density altitude

Everything in Thin Air depends on ρ. Prove you can compute it, and see how sensitive you are to it.

T = TISA + ΔT · ρ = p / (R T) · hρ = (288.15 / 0.0065) [1 − (ρ / 1.225)0.2350]

Reproduce ρ at DP-1, DP-2 and DP-3 within 0.5%, and give each density altitude. Then compute the percentage change in ideal hover power at DP-3 for a day 10 °C hotter (P ∝ ρ−½).

S-C2→ 2.1

Reynolds number & airfoil regime

Low Reynolds number changes everything: laminar bubbles, lower cl,max, higher drag.

Re = ρ V c / μ

At root and tip chords, at cruise (DP-2) and at wing-borne minimum speed (DP-1). Run or cite polars at these Re; report cl,max, cd at your cruise cl, cm,c/4, and the Ncrit you assumed.

Check: expect roughly 2 × 10⁵ to 6 × 10⁵.

S-C3→ 2.2

Wing sizing, wing loading & stall

Size the wing for cruise at DP-2 without making transition at DP-1 impossible.

W/S · AR = b²/S · c̄ = (2/3) cr (1 + λ + λ²)/(1 + λ) · CL = W/(qS) · Vstall = √(2W / (ρ S CL,max))

Give Vstall at DP-2 (for HR-7) and the wing-borne minimum speed at DP-1 (the speed transition must reach). Plot where your cruise CL sits on the polar.

Must meet: V ≥ 25 m/s and V ≥ 1.3 Vstall at DP-2; b ≤ 3.6 m.

S-C4→ 2.4

Zero-lift drag build-up & induced drag factor

Your drag must come from your aircraft, measured on your CAD model, not from a guess.

CD,0 = 1.08 × [ Σ (Cf,c FFc Qc Swet,c) + Σ (D/q)misc ] / Sref · K = 1/(π e AR)

One table row per component: length, Re, Cf, FF, Q, Swet (from CAD surface area), contribution. Show which components dominate and what you did about it.

S-C5→ 2.4

(L/D)max & characteristic speeds

Where is the aircraft most efficient, and is your cruise speed a deliberate choice?

(L/D)max = 1 / (2√(K CD,0)) · CL* = √(CD,0/K) · VL/D = √(2W / (ρ S CL*)) · VPmin = VL/D / 3¼

At DP-2. Plot power required against speed (MF-3). Explain why, with a 6 m/s headwind, the best-range speed is faster than VL/D.

S-C6→ 3.1

Hover power by momentum theory

Thin air punishes rotors. How many kilowatts does it take just to hang in the air at Tangtse?

Ti = k W / N · Pideal = Ti1.5 / √(2 ρ A) · Pshaft = Pideal / FM · Pelec = N Pshaft / (ηm ηESC)

A = π D²/4 per rotor, N rotors in hover. Give disk loading T/A. Compute at DP-1 and DP-3, and at 1.4 × hover thrust for HR-5 (power scales with T1.5).

S-C7→ 3.1

Motor–ESC–propeller matching

A motor that cannot spin the rotor fast enough at the loaded pack voltage cannot deliver HR-5, whatever its power rating says.

n = √(T / (CT ρ D⁴)) · rpm = 60 n ≤ 0.80 × KV × Vloaded · I = Pelec / Vloaded · Mtip = π n D / a

At 1.4 × hover thrust at DP-3. Check derated motor continuous power, ESC rating ≥ 1.25 I, and Mtip ≤ 0.60. Cite the motor datasheet.

S-C8→ 3.3

Climb & cruise power required

Climbing 2 km of altitude costs as much as the whole cruise. Count it.

Pcruise = D V / (ηp ηm ηESC) + Pav/0.90 · Pclimb = (D V + W Vv) / (ηp ηm ηESC) + Pav/0.90

Vv from the corridor geometry and your ground speed. Also give the maximum rate of climb available at DP-2 with your installed power.

Must meet: HR-12, climb ≥ 1.0 m/s at DP-2, and enough climb rate to follow the corridor.

S-C9→ 3.3

Mission energy budget & pack architecture

The single table that decides whether Thin Air reaches Tangtse.

E = Σ Pi ti · Eusable,req = E / 0.80 · Enom = Eusable,req / (0.90 fT) · Ns × Np

Rows: H1, T1, C, CR, D, L, approach, T2, H2, with time, power and energy in Wh. Choose cells, series count (voltage) and parallel count (capacity and current). Pack mass with 20% overhead.

Must meet: HR-4, ≥ 20% usable energy remaining at touchdown.

S-C10→ 3.4

Discharge rate, I²R heat & cell temperature

Hover at altitude is a high-current event. Cells have current and temperature limits before they have energy limits.

C-rate = Ipack / Ah · Icell = Ipack / Np · Q = Σ Icell² R t · ΔT = Q / (mcell cp) · Vloaded = Ns (Vcell − Icell R)

Segment by segment, adiabatic (no cooling credit) unless you model a cooling path. Show the cell temperature at the end of H2 from a 15 °C start.

Must meet: HR-10, 15–55 °C; Icell ≤ rated continuous current.

S-C11→ 4.3

CG, neutral point & static margin

A stable aircraft keeps its CG ahead of its neutral point, loaded, empty and at every tilt angle.

xcg from CAD mass properties · hn = hac + ηt V̄H (at/a)(1 − dε/dα) · V̄H = St lt / (S c̄) · dε/dα ≈ 2a / (π AR) · SM = hn − hcg

ηt ≈ 0.9; a and at per radian, finite-wing. XFLR5 neutral points are accepted if you show the model. Report SM for: payload in, payload out, 1.0 kg samples; and xcg in hover and cruise configurations for A and B.

Must meet: HR-8, 5–15% MAC in every wing-borne state.

S-C12→ 4.2

Spar bending, shear & tip deflection

The wing root carries the whole aircraft at 5.25 g. Size the spar to it.

nult = 1.5 × 3.5 · Mroot = (nult W / 2) × 4s / (3π) · σ = M c / I ≤ σallow · τ = V Q / (I t) · δtip ≈ w s⁴ / (8 E I)

s = semi-span; elliptic lift distribution (or Schrenk's approximation). For a tube spar, I = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/64; for caps and web, Acap ≈ M / (h σ). Deflection at limit load, uniform w = n W / b. Give MS for bending and shear.

Must meet: MS ≥ 0; tip deflection at limit load ≤ 10% of semi-span.

S-C13→ 2.5

Roll rate & aileron sizing

Ridge gusts roll the aircraft. Can the ailerons roll it back fast enough near stall?

p = −(Cl,δa / Cl,p) δa (2V/b) · Cl,δa = (2 CL,α τ / (S b)) ∫ c y dy · Cl,p ≈ −CL,α (1 + 3λ) / (12 (1 + λ))

Integrate over the aileron span. τ from aileron chord ratio: 0.20 → 0.41, 0.25 → 0.48, 0.30 → 0.52; δa,max = 20°. At 1.2 × wing-borne minimum speed at DP-1.

Target: bank 0° → 30° in ≤ 1.5 s (t ≈ φ / p).

S-C14→ 4.5

Wing-root joint & fastener check

HR-2 means the wing comes off. So the joint that carries 5.25 g must also be undone every day with a hex key.

F = Mroot / hj · Fbolt = F / nb · MSshear = Fallow / Fbolt − 1 · σbr = Fbolt / (d t) · MSbr = σbr,allow / σbr − 1

hj = distance between the bolt rows or lugs that form the couple. Show the joint in CD-2 with bolt size, grip length, edge distance (≥ 2d) and the hole tolerance you specify.

S-C15→ 4.4

Payload volumetric fraction & packaging

A 20.6-litre cold-chain box and a battery pack that warms up in every hover must share one fuselage without sharing heat.

fvol = Vpayload / Vfuselage,internal · Δx = xbox − xcg,empty · clearances from CD-3

Vfuselage,internal from CAD. Report the box's CG offset from the aircraft CG (and its effect in S-C11), the minimum clearance from box to pack and ESCs (HR-11), and the crate packing with the largest part's dimensions (HR-2).

Section 04 · Engineering dossier & CAD package architecture

No blank template.
An airframe architecture instead.

Why there is no fill-in-the-blank template

Earlier editions of Volatus handed out a document with headings to fill in: introduction, design phase, unique features, final design images. That works for a report about an aircraft. It does not work for the design of one. An airframe is an integrated system in which every decision reaches into every other one: sweep the wing and the neutral point moves aft, so the battery must move forward, so the nose gets longer, so the wetted area and the drag go up, so the battery must get bigger. A static text box labelled "Propulsion" cannot hold that loop.

Instead, this section gives you an architecture: the numbered sections every dossier must have, what each must contain, the drawings and figures the review board expects to find in each, the questions a good aircraft designer asks at each step, and page guidance by division. The design, the depth and the order in which you discovered things are yours. The numbering is fixed so judges can audit every dossier the same way.

A fill-in template gives you

  • Boxes sized for one kind of aircraft
  • Dossiers that look alike and hide their reasoning
  • "Final design images" instead of drawings
  • A reason to stop thinking once a box is full

This architecture gives you

  • A fixed numbering the jury audits line by line
  • Required content and CAD deliverables, not required answers
  • Guiding questions that expose the couplings
  • Division-specific depth and page guidance

By registering, every team member and the faculty advisor agree to these rules. R-01

Eligibility & teams

01.1
Volatus is a nationwide competition, open to students enrolled in Grades 6–12 at any recognised school in India during the 2026–27 academic year.
01.2
Junior Division: Grades 6–8. Senior Division: Grades 9–12. A student's grade on the Round 1 deadline decides eligibility.
01.3
A team has 2–4 students, all enrolled at the same school. A team with members from both grade ranges must enter the Senior Division.
01.4
A student may be a member of only one Volatus team. A school may enter more than one team; each team's work, including its CAD model, must be entirely independent.
01.5
Each team must have one faculty advisor: a teacher at the team's school who confirms eligibility, supervises preparation, countersigns the declaration, and is the organisers' point of contact.
01.6
Team membership is fixed at the Round 1 deadline. Members may withdraw afterwards, but no one may be added or replaced without the organisers' written approval.
01.7
Students involved in organising Volatus, or with access to RFP, curveball or judging materials before publication, may not enter.

Registration & communication

02.1
Teams register on the CelesteCon portal before the registration deadline published there, choosing their division and naming all members and the faculty advisor.
02.2
Each team receives a team registration code (for example J0417 or S0192). The code is used in the file name, in every CAD title block and anywhere the team must be identified inside the dossier, so that Round 1 can be judged double-blind.
02.3
All official communication goes to and from the faculty advisor's school email address. Messages from students or personal accounts are not treated as official requests.
02.4
Deadlines, venue details, fact sheet corrections and rule clarifications are published on the portal and emailed to advisors. Teams are responsible for checking both. All deadlines and times are Indian Standard Time (IST).

R-05

Originality & AI governance

Read in full before you start · applies to Round 1 and Round 2

This competition tests your team's own engineering judgement. The review board and the jury must be able to trust that the design, the words, the numbers, the drawings and the 3D geometry in your dossier and your presentation are yours.

This rule is deliberately specific so that no team is unsure what is allowed. If a use of a tool is not clearly listed as allowed below, treat it as not allowed, or ask the organisers in writing before the deadline.

5.1 What counts as "AI"

For this competition, AI means any software that generates, rewrites, completes, translates, summarises, designs, optimises or suggests content (text, numbers, tables, code, sketches, 3D geometry, drawings, images, audio, video or slides) instead of simply recording, displaying or calculating exactly what you typed, drew, modelled or entered. This includes AI built into everyday apps and CAD packages, whether or not the feature is labelled "AI".

It counts as using AI whether you typed a prompt, clicked a button such as "Generate", "Rewrite", "Improve", "Summarise", "Magic Design", "Help me write", "Generative design" or "Auto-complete sketch", accepted a suggested phrase, or let a CAD or solver feature create or reshape geometry for you. Not knowing that a feature uses AI is not a defence.

Autonomy and on-board intelligence as part of your aircraft design (for example an obstacle detection algorithm) are engineering content and are welcome. This rule is about the tools you use to produce your dossier, your model and your presentation.

5.3 The three-tier integrity model

Tier 1
Permitted standard tools

Ordinary tools that record, draw, calculate or check your own work. No declaration needed beyond listing the software and version in 7.4.

  • Parametric CAD (Fusion, SolidWorks, Onshape, FreeCAD, Inventor, CATIA, Solid Edge, Creo) where your team creates every sketch, feature, constraint and dimension, with generative and AI features switched off or unused.
  • Deterministic aerodynamic and engineering solvers: XFLR5, XFOIL, JavaFoil, OpenVSP, OpenRocket, eCalc-style propulsion calculators, FEA and CFD solvers, and the calculators on this page, run on geometry and inputs your team set up.
  • Importing published airfoil coordinates (for example from the UIUC database) as spline points, credited.
  • Official manufacturer CAD of bought-in parts (motors, servos, connectors, fasteners), credited and not modified to look like your own design.
  • Spreadsheets with formulas your team wrote; native word processing with AI features off; basic spell-check, accepted one word at a time.
  • Manual web research, citation managers for sources you found, and diagram tools where your team places every element.
Tier 2
Permitted with mandatory disclosure

Limited research help. Every use must be recorded in the Section 7.4 disclosure ledger: tool, date, what you asked, and how you used the answer.

  • Conceptual background research: asking an AI chatbot to explain a concept (for example, "what is a figure of merit?") that you then study from a real source. You may not copy or paraphrase its answer.
  • Research paper discovery: using AI search to find papers, reports or datasheets, as long as you open, read and cite the original, never the AI tool, for every fact you use.
  • Formula and software troubleshooting: asking why a spreadsheet formula your team wrote returns an error, or how a CAD command or XFLR5 setting works.
  • Checking a finished calculation: asking an AI tool whether your team's completed working has an error. The method, the model and the numbers must remain your own.
Tier 3
Strictly prohibited

Any use in which AI produces, rewrites, designs or decides part of your submission or presentation.

  • AI generating CAD or geometry: text-to-3D, image-to-3D, text-to-CAD, generative design, AI topology optimisation, AI-completed sketches, AI "optimised" airfoils or wings.
  • AI generating designs: configurations, layouts, trade-study criteria or scores, risk registers, names or mission plans.
  • AI writing or rewriting text: any sentence, heading, caption or table entry, and any AI rewriting, "improving", summarising or translating of text your team wrote.
  • AI producing engineering numbers: asking AI for a lift coefficient, a motor efficiency, a battery mass or a spar size and using it. AI-supplied figures are often invented ("hallucinated") and are treated as fabricated data.
  • AI images, renders or slides: AI rendering, AI-edited images, AI-drawn diagrams, AI-designed slide decks or templates.
  • Humanizer, paraphrasing or detection-avoidance tools on any text: treated as deliberate concealment.
  • Any AI tool or connected device during the Round 2 flight review.

5.4 Common claims, and how this rule treats them

"We modelled the wing ourselves but used generative design to lighten the ribs."
Tier 3
Prohibited. Generated or AI-optimised geometry is AI-designed geometry. Model the lightening holes yourselves and justify them.
"We imported NACA 2412 coordinates from the UIUC database and lofted the wing."
Tier 1
Allowed. Published coordinates are data, not AI. Credit the source in 7.3.
"We downloaded the motor's STEP file from the manufacturer's website."
Tier 1
Allowed, credited, and shown as a bought-in part in the BOM. Its mount, the airframe around it and its placement are yours.
"We asked a chatbot for the CL,max of our airfoil and used it."
Tier 3
An engineering value taken from AI is prohibited. Run XFLR5 or find wind-tunnel data and cite that.
"AI found us two NASA reports on low-Reynolds airfoils, which we read and cited."
Tier 2
Allowed, if declared in 7.4 and every fact is cited from the reports themselves.
"We ran our own wing through XFLR5 and a CFD solver."
Tier 1
Allowed. Deterministic solvers compute what you set up. State the settings; list the software in 7.4.
"We only used it to fix grammar."
Depends
Correcting single misspelled words is Tier 1. Accepting a rewrite of any phrase or sentence is Tier 3.
"We didn't know our CAD package's sketch tool auto-suggested geometry."
No defence
Check your tools' features before you use them, or ask the organisers before the deadline.
"A detector flagged us, but we didn't use AI."
Review
A detector score alone never decides a case. You will be asked to show your working, your CAD history and to explain it (5.6).

5.5 Plagiarism and help from others

a
Copying or closely paraphrasing text, tables, figures, drawings, CAD geometry or data from any source without quotation marks (for text) and a citation is plagiarism.
b
Reusing another team's work, a previous year's entry, a published student competition entry, a downloaded hobby model (for example from a model-sharing website) or a commercial aircraft design as your own is plagiarism, even if it has been changed. Reference designs may be cited and compared, never presented as yours.
c
A team member's own earlier work may be reused only if current team members created it, it is cited, and it is declared in 7.4. The dossier and model must still be substantially new work answering this RFP.
d
Teachers, parents, mentors and professionals may discuss ideas, ask questions and give general feedback. They may not write, edit, calculate, draw, model or design any part of the dossier, model or slides. Significant feedback must be described in 7.4.
e
Paid or professional services (writers, consultants, CAD modellers, drafters, renderers, tutors producing content) are not allowed.

TECHNICAL FAQQuestions teams ask

Eligibility, teams & format
Our team has one Grade 8 student and three Grade 9 students. Which division do we enter?

Senior. A team with members from both grade ranges must enter the Senior Division (R-01.3). Grades are counted on the Round 1 deadline.

Is this still "Dimension III"? Can we enter Dimension III separately?

From Edition VI, Dimension III is part of Volatus and is not run as a separate contest. Every Volatus team is judged on its 3D CAD work through the Mechanical CAD Modelling & Drafting criterion (20 of the 100 Round 1 points), the live CAD interrogation in Round 2 (25 of the 100 Round 2 points), and the Master Modeller Prize. Teams whose strength is 3D design will find it counts for more than ever, but only when it is engineering, not decoration.

Can we answer the other division's RFP, or combine elements of both?

No. Each division answers only its own RFP, with its own fact sheet, so the jury compares like with like. You may, of course, read the other RFP and learn from it.

CAD, drawings & the 3D package
Which CAD software and file formats are accepted?

Any parametric solid modeller used as a Tier 1 tool: Fusion, SolidWorks, Onshape (free education plans work well), FreeCAD, Inventor, CATIA, Solid Edge or Creo. In Round 1 nothing but the PDF is uploaded: drawings exported as vector PDF from the CAD drawing workspace, and figures inside the PDF. For Round 2 finalists upload one STEP assembly (AP214 or AP242). Keep the native file with its full history for audit. Blender, SketchUp or mesh tools may be used for renders, but not for CD-1 to CD-4.

Do we have to model every screw and wire?

You must model every fastener in primary structure and in joints that come apart (wing root, tail, motor mount, payload restraint), at its true size, and name it in the BOM. Small repeated fasteners in secondary structure may be shown once and listed with a quantity. The main power wires and the harness route must be modelled in CD-3; signal wires may be shown as a bundle. Judges are checking whether things fit, so anything that takes space, carries load or carries current must be there.

How do we make a custom airfoil, or modify a standard one?

Design or modify it in XFLR5 or XFOIL (for example changing thickness, camber or adding reflex), analyse it at your Reynolds number, export the coordinates, and import them into CAD as a spline. In 2.1, show the geometry changes, the polars before and after, and why the change is worth it. An "AI optimised" airfoil is Tier 3 and prohibited; one you shaped and analysed yourselves is fine.

Can we use a manufacturer's CAD model for our motor or flight controller?

Yes. Official CAD of bought-in parts is Tier 1, as long as it is credited in 7.3 and marked as a bought-in part in the BOM. If none exists, model the part from the datasheet's dimensioned drawing. What is scored is how you integrate it: the mount, the clearances, the cooling, the wiring.

First-angle or third-angle projection?

Either, as long as you declare it with the correct ISO projection symbol in the title block and arrange the views accordingly. Many aerospace drawings use third-angle; Indian standards commonly teach first-angle. Mixing the two on one sheet is a drafting error.

Aerodynamics, analysis & software
Do we need CFD? We don't have access to ANSYS or similar.

No. CFD is never required. XFLR5 (free) covers airfoil polars, wing lift distribution, stability derivatives and neutral point estimates for both divisions, and is enough for CD-4. If you do use CFD, you must state the solver, mesh, turbulence model, boundary conditions and a comparison with your hand calculation, or it earns nothing. A clearly explained XFLR5 analysis beats an unexplained colourful CFD image every time.

Our XFLR5 CL,max is different from the airfoil card on this page. Which do we use?

Use your own analysis at your own Reynolds number, state the settings (Re, Ncrit, number of panels), and record the difference in 7.1. The airfoil cards are indicative starting points for choosing, not values to quote. Remember that the whole aircraft reaches about 90% of the section cl,max.

Senior: can we use our own ISA spreadsheet instead of the design atmosphere table?

Yes, S-C1 asks you to reproduce the table. If your values differ by more than 0.5%, find out why before you use them; the fact-sheet values are the competition baseline.

3D printing, prototypes & Round 2
Should we design for 3D printing or for traditional composite and balsa construction?

Either, as long as you justify it in 4.5 and 6.1. 3D printing is fast and precise but heavy and weak across layers (use the fact-sheet knockdown), and PLA softens in the sun. Foam, balsa, plywood and carbon tube are light and strong but need jigs and skill. Many good designs mix them: printed fittings and ducts, a carbon spar, foam or balsa ribs, a film or glass skin. Show the mass consequence of your choice.

Do we need to build a physical model for Round 2?

No. Models are optional and are not scored for craftsmanship (R-07). A full-size sub-assembly, such as a wing section with its real spar and ribs or a working release mechanism, often helps a defence more than a small scale model of the whole aircraft. No lithium batteries, no propellers on motors, and nothing flies at the venue.

What exactly happens in the live CAD interrogation?

Your STEP file, uploaded 48 hours before, is opened on the venue workstation in a neutral viewer. One of your team members drives. Jurors ask for things such as: "hide the skin", "section at the wing spar", "measure the gap between the battery and the payload", "show the root joint bolts", "rotate the tilt axis". Practise with a free STEP viewer and export a test STEP early: some exports lose colours, part names or assembly structure, and 12.3 means that is your risk.

We found an error in our dossier after submission. Can we fix it for Round 2?

Your Round 1 score is fixed. In Round 2 you may present corrected figures and an updated STEP model if a slide or answer explains each change and its reason (R-06.10). Jurors respect teams that find and fix their own mistakes.

AI, originality & help
Our CAD software has an AI assistant built in. Can we use it to answer "how do I" questions?

Asking how a command works is Tier 2 (software troubleshooting) and must be logged in 7.4. Letting it create, complete, constrain or modify any sketch or feature is Tier 3 and prohibited. If in doubt, use the software's written documentation instead.

How do we record a Tier 2 AI use in 7.4?

One row per use: date, tool, the exact question you asked (or a faithful summary), what you did with the answer, and the original source you then read and cited for any fact. For example: "12 Aug, [tool], asked what 'figure of merit' means for a rotor; read Leishman (2006), ch. 2, and cited it in 3.1." Never paste AI text into the dossier.

A professional engineer in our family offered to review our dossier. Is that allowed?

They may ask questions and give general feedback ("have you checked the spar in compression?"), which you must describe in 7.4. They may not write, edit, calculate, draw or model any part of it (R-05.5d). The test: every word, number, line and feature must have been produced by a team member.

Still unsure? Your faculty advisor can write to the AEROSS organising committee through the contact on the CelesteCon portal, from the school email address, before the Round 1 deadline. Answers that affect other teams are shared with all advisors.
