01

Section 01 · Premise

The brief: fun is never an accident

In game design, as in aerospace engineering, the result that people feel is the consequence of rules that someone chose carefully. A landing that feels tense is tense because the propellant gauge, the thrust-to-weight ratio and the descent rate are pulling against each other in a way the player can read. An orbit that feels beautiful is beautiful because the integrator conserves energy and the camera reveals the geometry. Fun is not sprinkled on afterwards. It emerges from elegant rules, tight mechanical loops and constraints that are real enough to reason about.

Vector GameJam asks school studios to build that kind of game. Your team will choose a mechanic rooted in orbital mechanics, flight dynamics or spacecraft systems, model it honestly in code, wrap it in an interface a pilot would recognise, tune it until a first-time player understands it within two minutes, and then defend it: the maths on paper, the architecture in the source, and the build on a public kiosk in front of engineers who will try to break it.

A beautiful game that cheats its physics loses to a plainer game whose team can defend every integrator, every state transition and every millisecond of frame time.

What your studio actually ships

Every team founds a small game studio: a named team with roles, a production schedule, a bug tracker and a risk register. The studio ships one complete, playable game built around its division's challenge, with a start screen, a tutorial or onboarding sequence, at least the required number of levels or mission phases, a win and a fail condition, settings for audio and controls, and a credits screen that lists every third-party asset and licence.

Alongside the build, the studio writes a Game Design Document of professional standard: the core loop, the physics model and its derivations, the software architecture and state machines, the level design and difficulty curve, the art, audio and HUD design, the production history and the performance profile. The source code travels with it, complete with version-control history, so that reviewers can see how the game was built and not only what it became.

Why real physics makes better games

Players are extraordinary pattern detectors. When a lander accelerates the same way at every altitude, when an orbit fails to close, or when drag vanishes the moment the frame rate drops, players feel it before they can name it, and the game loses its authority. When the rules are true, the game can afford to be hard, because every failure is fair and every success is learnable. Real aerospace constraints are also the richest source of interesting decisions: burn now or coast, save propellant or save time, bank harder to shed speed or ease off to save the heat shield. Those trade-offs are gameplay.

What the jury will check first

  • That the core mechanic changes when the physics changes: double the gravity and the game must play differently, not just look different.
  • That physics runs on a fixed timestep, independent of frame rate, and that the team can show the accumulator in its own code.
  • That every number on the HUD comes from the simulation state and means what its label says.
  • That the build starts, runs at a steady 60 frames per second on the kiosk, survives a pause, a restart and a controller disconnect, and never crashes to desktop.
  • That every asset, library and tool is declared, and that the team wrote every line of gameplay code it claims to have written.
T1

The aerospace mechanics test

Does the core mechanic model real aerodynamic, orbital or spacecraft-systems physics, rather than decorating a generic genre with space art?

  • Forces summed as vectors each step: thrust, gravity, drag and lift where they apply.
  • Mass that changes as propellant burns, and consequences the player can feel.
  • Parameters traceable to the division fact sheet or to a cited source.

Fails if: the game would play identically with the physics removed, or constants are tuned by feel with no stated model behind them.

T2

The software architecture & performance test

Is the game engineered: clean state management, deterministic physics ticks, steady frame pacing and no leaks?

  • A fixed physics step with an accumulator and a clamp against the spiral of death.
  • Explicit game and entity state machines, with every transition named and handled.
  • 60 FPS on kiosk hardware; memory flat across ten minutes of play and three restarts.

Fails if: physics runs in the render update with a variable step, or memory grows every time a level restarts.

T3

The game feel & player agency test

Can a first-time player understand what to do, feel in control, and see why they succeeded or failed?

  • Clear feedback loops: every input answered within one frame by sound, motion or HUD change.
  • Visual hierarchy that puts the critical reading where the eye already is.
  • Intuitive, remappable controls and a difficulty curve that teaches before it tests.

Fails if: a juror cannot complete the first objective within two minutes without being told the controls by the team.

T4

The code defence & scrutineering test

Can every member of the team open the project, find a script, explain it, change it and rebuild it, live?

  • Formulas in the GDD matched line by line to functions in the source.
  • State machines, signals and event flows explained from the code, not from memory.
  • A commit history that shows the game growing over weeks, with authorship spread across the team.

Fails if: the team cannot locate its own integrator, or the history shows the project appearing fully formed in a single commit.

1.1What this is, and what it is not

Not this

  • A platformer, runner or shooter with rockets and stars painted on, where gravity is a jump arc tuned by feel.
  • Velocity set directly from key presses, so that the craft stops the instant the player lets go.
  • A HUD of decorative numbers that do not come from the simulation.
  • Physics that changes with frame rate, so the game is easier on a slow laptop.
  • A template project, tutorial scene or asset-store kit with a new title screen.
  • Code, art, music or text generated by AI and presented as the team's work.

This

  • A core mechanic that is the physics: burns, drag, gravity wells, heat, power and propellant.
  • Forces summed and integrated on a fixed step, with momentum the player must manage.
  • Avionics-grade HUD readouts, warnings and annunciators driven by real state.
  • Deterministic simulation: the same inputs produce the same flight on any machine.
  • An original game with a design document, a ledger of derivations and a history of commits.
  • A studio that can open any file, explain any line and patch any system on the spot.

1.3What wins at Vector GameJam

A

Mechanical depth

A core loop with real decisions in it, where the physics creates the tension and the player's skill grows with understanding, not with memorised patterns.

Judge asks: "What decision does a player make every ten seconds, and what physics makes it hard?"

B

Technical architecture

A deterministic simulation separated from rendering, explicit state machines, data-driven parameters, steady frame pacing and a codebase the whole team can navigate.

Judge asks: "Show me where the accumulator is clamped, and tell me why that number."

C

Art & audio cohesion

Visuals, sound and interface that belong to one world and serve play: a HUD that reads at a glance, audio that carries information, and effects that explain the physics.

Judge asks: "Turn the sound off. What information did the player just lose?"

D

Thematic verisimilitude

A game that an aerospace engineer would recognise as honest: correct units, credible parameters, simplifications that are declared and defended, and nothing that contradicts the science.

Judge asks: "Where did you simplify the physics, and what would a real pilot notice?"

1.4The performance envelope

Junior envelope

60 frames per second at 1280 × 720 or higher on the kiosk reference machine; physics at a fixed 60 Hz; cold start to first playable frame within 20 s; web build download ≤ 150 MB; browser memory ≤ 1 GB.

tframe ≤ 1/60 s = 16.67 ms · Δtphys = 16.67 ms

Senior envelope

60 frames per second at 1920 × 1080 on the kiosk; proximity and entry physics at a fixed 120 Hz or faster; 99th-percentile frame time ≤ 20 ms; cold start within 30 s; offline package ≤ 2 GB; process memory ≤ 3 GB.

p99(tframe) ≤ 20 ms · Δtphys ≤ 8.33 ms

How it is measured

Every build must include a toggleable performance overlay (frame time, FPS, physics steps per frame and memory) bound to F3. Reviewers use it in Round 1; the kiosk marshal records it at the Round 2 smoke test and during juror playtests. Profiler captures from your own testing belong in GDD §6.3.

The envelope is a floor, not a target for visual ambition. A modest-looking game that holds 60 frames per second with a clean fixed step outscores a lavish one that stutters, because stutter corrupts the very physics the game is about.

Section 02 · Release pipeline

Format, timeline & scoring

Vector GameJam follows the CelesteCon two-round framework. Round 1 is asynchronous and nationwide: the build is played, the source is read and the Game Design Document is judged, all double-blind, by a review board of game developers and aerospace and simulation engineers. Round 2 is live and on campus: the game must run on a public kiosk, survive strangers, and be defended and patched by the people who built it.

What the Expo crowd does and does not decide

Visitors on the Arcade Expo floor play the games and vote for the People's Playtest Choice, but their votes are not part of the 200 points. Juror playtests are. The difference matters: a crowd rewards spectacle in the first thirty seconds, while a juror playtest measures whether a stranger can learn the game, whether the physics holds up under unexpected play, and whether the build stays stable for ten minutes of deliberate abuse.

Tie-breaks

If two studios finish on the same combined total, the tie is broken in this order: higher Round 2 curveball sprint score; then higher Round 1 Calculations & Physics Ledger Integrity score; then fewer kiosk stability penalty points; then higher Round 1 Software Architecture & Code Quality score.

Section 03 · Problem statements

The division challenges

Each division has its own challenge, mission requirements, fact sheet and mandatory calculations. Read your own division in full, then read the other one: Junior studios will find the Senior reasoning useful, and Senior studios will be asked in scrutineering to explain their systems as simply as a Junior team would.

All numbers in the fact sheets are CelesteCon reference values published for this competition. They are the parameters your game must use by default. You may add difficulty settings that scale them, provided the default setting uses the published values and the GDD states every scale factor.

Challenge J · Grades 6–8 · Junior division

Rim Runner

Fly rescue cargo across the lunar south pole, then bring a descent pod down through the thin air of Mars.

GJ-JJunior brief · Edition VI

A rover team has lost power in the permanent shadow of a crater near the Moon's south pole. The only craft close enough to help is a small propulsive hopper parked on the sunlit rim, and you are its pilot. You have a tank of propellant, a battery that the Sun can only recharge on the rim, and three cargo pods of spare batteries, oxygen and tools. Every hop costs fuel. Every minute in the shadow costs power. Every pod you carry makes the hopper heavier and harder to stop.

When the rescue is done, the same crew must go to Mars, where the rules change. There is air, thin but real, which means drag, a terminal velocity and a parachute that can save you or tear itself apart if you open it too fast. Gravity is more than twice as strong. The descent pod must shed about 260 metres per second before it touches the ground, and the player decides how much of that is done by the air and how much by the engines.

The game

Your studio builds a flight game with at least three levels: a training level on a flat pad, a crater relay with at least three hops, and a Mars descent. The hopper and the pod obey the forces in the fact sheet, integrated on a fixed 1/60 s step. The player controls throttle and attitude, reads a HUD that shows the truth, and wins by landing gently enough, in the right place, with resources left.

What makes this hard

Thrust points where the hopper points, so tilting to move sideways also reduces the upward push. The hopper's mass falls as propellant burns and rises when it picks up cargo, so the same throttle behaves differently at the start and end of a level. On Mars, drag grows with the square of speed and depends on altitude. A game that gets these right feels alive; a game that fakes them feels floaty and random.

Your studio must

  • Name the studio and assign 2–4 roles, for example lead programmer, physics and systems designer, artist and UI designer, and producer and sound designer.
  • Build the flight model from the fact sheet on a fixed timestep.
  • Ship three levels: Pad School, Crater Relay and Red Drop.
  • Run propellant, battery and cargo as systems the player must manage.
  • Design a HUD with altitude, speeds, propellant, power, attitude and warnings.
  • Complete calculations J-C1 to J-C10 in the GDD ledger.
  • Keep the project in version control from the first week.

Deliverables at a glance

  • Round 1: GDD ≤ 16 main-body pages, playable build, source archive, capture video, asset register.
  • Round 2: kiosk build, ≤ 10-slide deck, change log, development laptop for the sprint.

A great Junior entry

Has a hopper that feels heavy with cargo and light without it; a HUD a player trusts after one landing; a first level that teaches without a wall of text; honest screenshots of early builds in the GDD; and a team in which every member can point to the code they wrote.

J.1Mission requirements

JR-1

Found the studio

Create a game studio with a name, a visual identity and clear roles.

  • Studio name, logo and game title that are your own and do not copy a real company, game or trademark.
  • A roles table: who owned which system, who reviewed whose work, and which commits each role authored.
Goes in  §0.1, §6.1
JR-2

Build the flight model

Sum the forces on the craft as vectors and integrate them on a fixed step.

  • Thrust along the craft's own axis, gravity straight down, drag against the velocity on Mars.
  • Mass = dry mass + propellant + cargo, updated every step as propellant burns.
  • Semi-implicit Euler at Δt = 1/60 s with an accumulator and a clamp of five steps per frame.
  • Engine rigid bodies may handle contacts, but every flight force must be applied by your own code.
Goes in  §2.1, §3.2, J-C1, J-C6
JR-3

Three levels that teach, then test

Pad School, Crater Relay and Red Drop, in that order, as specified in J.2.

  • Each level introduces at most one new idea and lets the player practise it before it matters.
  • Checkpoints on landing pads; a restart that returns the level to its exact starting state.
  • A results screen showing time, propellant used, landing speeds and a star or medal rating.
Goes in  §4.0, GJM-CD-4
JR-4

Resource systems

Propellant, battery power and cargo mass that interact and create decisions.

  • Propellant flow proportional to throttle; an empty tank means no thrust, not a game-over screen.
  • Battery drained by avionics, radar and lights and recharged by the Sun only in sunlit zones.
  • Cargo pods that add 50 kg each, so that the player feels the hover throttle rise.
Goes in  §2.3, J-C3, J-C4
JR-5

Landing, collision and damage

Decide how contact works and make every outcome readable.

  • Landing judged on vertical speed, horizontal speed, tilt and pad slope, using the fact-sheet limits.
  • Collision response by impulse with restitution and friction, not by snapping the craft to the ground.
  • Three outcomes, safe, hard and crash, each with distinct sound, animation and HUD message.
Goes in  §2.2, J-C8, J-C9
JR-6

HUD and avionics

Give the pilot the instruments a real lander would have, driven by real state.

  • Radar altitude from a raycast to the terrain, vertical and horizontal speed, attitude, throttle, propellant, battery and thrust-to-weight.
  • Warning annunciators: LOW FUEL, LOW POWER, SINK RATE, TILT and, on Mars, CHUTE LIMIT.
  • A burn-altitude cue that tells the player when a full-thrust stop would begin (J-C10).
Goes in  §5.3, GJM-CD-3
JR-7

Game states and architecture

An explicit state machine and a code structure the whole team understands.

  • States at least: Boot, Title, Briefing, Flying, Paused, Landed, Crashed, Level Complete, Settings and Credits, with every transition drawn in GJM-CD-1.
  • Parameters in one data file or resource, not scattered through scripts.
  • Restarting a level three times must not increase memory use.
Goes in  §3.0, GJM-CD-1
JR-8

Game feel, audio and accessibility

J.4Mandatory calculations · physics ledger J-C1 to J-C10

Every calculation goes in the GDD §7.2 ledger with inputs, formula, working, result with units, a check, and the file and function where your game implements it. The reference answers below use the fact-sheet values. Your numbers will match where you used the same inputs; your method must match everywhere.

J-C1FRAME

Frame budget and fixed timestep

A frame is the time the computer has to read input, update the game and draw it. Physics runs on its own fixed clock, so you must know how many physics steps each frame will run.

tframe = 1 / fdisplay · steps = ⌊acc / Δt⌋ · acc ← acc − steps·Δt

Work it for 30, 60 and 144 frames per second, with Δt = 1/60 s.

Ref: 60 Hz → 16.67 ms, 1 step per frame; 30 Hz → 33.33 ms, 2 steps; 144 Hz → 6.94 ms, a step on about 5 frames in every 12 (60/144 = 0.417).

J-C2DELTA

Frame-rate independence

If movement is added per frame, a faster computer makes the game faster. Scaling by the elapsed time fixes it. Prove this with numbers, then prove your game does it.

per-frame: x = nframes · 0.1 m · delta-time: x = Σ v·Δt = v·t

A craft moving at 5.0 m/s for 1.0 s, at 30, 60 and 144 frames per second.

Ref: per-frame gives 3.0 m, 6.0 m and 14.4 m; delta-time gives 5.0 m at every frame rate.

J-C3T/W

Thrust-to-weight and hover throttle

Thrust-to-weight above 1 means the hopper can climb. Its inverse is the throttle needed to hover, which changes as propellant burns and cargo is loaded.

T/W = T / (m·g) · throttlehover = 1 / (T/W)

Give values for wet, dry, and wet with one cargo pod, at kT = 1.00.

Ref: wet 2.47 (hover 40.5%); dry 3.70 (27.0%); wet + pod, 650 kg, 2.28 (43.9%). Commander kT 0.85, wet: 2.10.

J-C4ENDURE

Hover endurance and battery endurance

How long can the hopper hover before the tank is empty, and how long can it stay in the shadow before the battery dies? These two clocks shape the whole Crater Relay.

ṁhover = mmean·g / ve · thover = mprop / ṁhover · tbat = Ebat / Pnet

Use the mean of wet and dry mass. For the battery, compare shadow (60 + 40 + 20 W) with sunlit (60 + 40 − 80 W).

Ref: mmean 500 kg → ṁ 0.3375 kg/s → 592.6 s (exact exponential answer 600.7 s). Battery: shadow 300 s; sunlit net 20 W → 1,800 s.

J-C5HOP

Ballistic hop vector

With the engine off, the hopper is a projectile. Splitting velocity into x and y parts predicts where a hop lands, which is how your trajectory-preview line should work.

vx = v0cosθ · vy = v0sinθ · R = v0²sin2θ / g · t = 2v0sinθ / g · hmax = vy² / 2g

Launch at 20.0 m/s and 45° from level ground on the Moon; compare the range on Earth.

Ref: vx = vy = 14.14 m/s; R = 246.9 m; t = 17.46 s; hmax = 61.73 m. On Earth R = 40.8 m.

J-C6INTEGRATE

Integrator error check

The order of two lines of code changes the physics. Compare explicit Euler (position first) with semi-implicit Euler (velocity first) against the exact answer.

explicit: x += v·Δt; v += g·Δt · semi-implicit: v += g·Δt; x += v·Δt · exact: x = ½gt²

Free fall from rest on the Moon for 1.0 s at Δt = 1/60 s (60 steps). Show your working as a sum.

Ref: exact 0.8100 m; explicit g·Δt²·Σ(0…59) = 0.7965 m (−1.67%); semi-implicit g·Δt²·Σ(1…60) = 0.8235 m (+1.67%).

J-C7ATMOS

Mars density and terminal velocity

Thin air still matters when you fall fast enough. Terminal velocity is the speed at which drag equals weight, and it changes with altitude because density does.

ρ(h) = ρ0e−h/H · vt = √(2mg / (ρ·Σ CDA))

Find ρ at 10,000 m, then vt for the pod at 10,000 m and at the surface, without and with the parachute.

Ref: ρ(10 km) = 0.00812 kg/m³; vt 269.6 m/s at 10 km; 171.8 m/s at the surface; with parachute at the surface 64.9 m/s.

J-C8ENERGY

Mechanical energy as a debug invariant

With the engine off and no air, kinetic plus potential energy must stay constant. Your game can check this every step and flag a bug if it drifts.

E = ½mv² + mgh · crash test: v = √(2gh) · KEtouchdown = ½mv²

For the J-C5 hop with a 450 kg hopper, find E at launch and at the apex. Then find the impact speed after an engine-off fall of 20 m.

Ref: E = 90,000 J at launch; apex KE 45,000 J + PE 45,000 J. Fall from 20 m: 8.05 m/s, a crash. KE at 2.0 m/s is 900 J; at 4.0 m/s, 3,600 J.

J-C9IMPULSE

Collision impulse and friction

A good collision response changes velocity by an impulse along the contact normal, with friction limiting the sideways change. This is what makes a bounce feel solid.

Jn = m(1 + e)|vn| · vn′ = −e·vn · |Jt| ≤ μJn · Favg = Jn / Δt

A 450 kg hopper strikes flat regolith at 3.0 m/s straight down.

Ref: Jn = 1,620 N·s; rebound 0.6 m/s up; over one step Favg = 97,200 N (22.0 Earth g); friction impulse ≤ 972 N·s, so sideways Δv ≤ 2.16 m/s.

J-C10BURN

Retro-burn ignition altitude

The most efficient landing lights the engine as late as possible at full thrust. Your HUD cue must know the stopping distance, plus a safety margin.

anet = T/m − g · d = v² / (2anet) · t = v / anet

A 450 kg hopper descending at 12.0 m/s on the Moon, full thrust, pointing straight up. Add a 20% margin for the HUD cue.

Ref: anet = 3.713 m/s²; d = 19.39 m; t = 3.23 s; HUD cue at 23.3 m.

Challenge S · Grades 9–12 · Senior division

Terminal Phase

Chase a space station across low Earth orbit, dock within centimetres per second, then bring the crew home through the fire.

GJ-SSenior brief · Edition VI

The crew ferry Kestrel has just separated from its launcher in a 200 km parking orbit. The research station Aurora is 30 degrees ahead, 220 km higher, and moving slower. To catch it, the pilot must first fall further behind in angle, then climb; to approach it, the pilot must thrust towards it and watch the ferry drift away. Orbital mechanics is counter-intuitive, precise and unforgiving. That is exactly why it makes a great game.

After docking and a crew exchange, Kestrel undocks, burns retrograde and falls into the atmosphere at nearly 7.9 km/s. Now the enemy is heat. Fly the capsule lift-up and it lofts, stays hot for too long and burns through the ablator. Roll it over and it dives, pulls more g than the crew can take, and flares past the heat-flux limit. Somewhere between is a corridor, and the player must find it through a plasma blackout with no word from the ground.

The game

Your studio builds a spaceflight simulation game in five mission phases, with a team-written orbital propagator, relative-motion guidance aids, a layered atmosphere, a drag polar with bank-angle lift steering, Sutton–Graves heating, spacecraft systems that can fail, and telemetry the player can plot and export. Presentation is part of the engineering: the HUD must look and behave like avionics, and at least one shader must be written by the team and driven by physical state.

What makes this hard

Everything is coupled. Burning propellant changes mass and moves the centre of mass, which changes how the RCS rotates the ferry. Time warp tempts you to lengthen the physics step, which breaks energy conservation. Positions of seven million metres destroy single-precision floats at exactly the scale where docking happens. The best Senior entries treat these as design problems to be solved in the architecture, not bugs to be patched in the last week.

Your studio must

  • Name the studio and assign 2–4 roles, for example technical director, simulation engineer, gameplay and UI engineer, and technical artist and audio designer.
  • Write its own orbital propagator and flight-dynamics model; engine gravity and rigid-body integration may not be used for flight.
  • Ship all five mission phases, P1 to P5, with P2 and P4 fully simulated.
  • Implement power, thermal and life-support loops that can fail.
  • Log at least 32 telemetry channels at 50 Hz and export them as CSV.
  • Complete calculations S-C1 to S-C14 in the GDD ledger.
  • Maintain automated tests for the physics core.

Deliverables at a glance

  • Round 1: GDD ≤ 26 main-body pages, playable build, source archive with history, capture video, asset register, a sample telemetry CSV.
  • Round 2: kiosk build, ≤ 12-slide deck, change log, development laptop for the sprint.

A great Senior entry

Has an integrator whose energy drift is measured and plotted in the GDD; a docking that is hard because of Clohessy–Wiltshire dynamics, not because of bad controls; an entry corridor the player can learn; a plasma shader driven by heat flux; and a replay system that proves the simulation is deterministic.

S.4Mandatory calculations · physics ledger S-C1 to S-C14

Every calculation goes in the GDD §7.2 ledger with inputs, method, working, result with units, a check, and the source file, function and commit where your game implements it. Where a calculation is also computed by your game, include the in-game value and explain any difference. The reference answers below use the fact-sheet values.

S-C1ORBIT

Circular orbit speed, period and mean motion

The two orbits define the whole rendezvous. Mean motion n sets the time scale of proximity operations in S-C6.

v = √(μ/r) · T = 2π√(r³/μ) · n = 2π/T

For the 200 km parking orbit and the 420 km station orbit.

Ref: 200 km: 7,784.3 m/s, 88.49 min. 420 km: 7,657.3 m/s, 5,578.2 s = 92.97 min, n = 1.1264 × 10⁻³ rad/s.

S-C2TRANSFER

Hohmann transfer, lead angle and phasing wait

A transfer only meets the station if the station is in the right place when the ferry arrives. The phasing wait is why the game needs time warp.

Δv₁ = v₁(√(2r₂/(r₁+r₂)) − 1) · Δv₂ = v₂(1 − √(2r₁/(r₁+r₂))) · tT = π√(aT³/μ) · θlead = π(1 − (aT/r₂)3/2)

Then the wait: tw = (θ0 − θlead) / (ω₁ − ω₂).

Ref: Δv₁ 63.75, Δv₂ 63.23, total 126.98 m/s; tT 2,721.7 s (45.36 min); θlead 4.35°; ω₁ − ω₂ = 0.003264 °/s; tw = 7,857 s (2.18 h).

S-C3ROCKET

Rocket equation, mass ratio and Δv budget

Propellant is the currency of the whole mission. The Δv budget decides how many mistakes a player can afford.

Δv = Ispg0·ln(m0/mf) · mprop = m0(1 − e−Δv/(Ispg0))

Capacity of Kestrel on OMS; then the propellant for transfer plus deorbit (S-C2 and 107.86 m/s) with a 25% margin.

Ref: c = 3,098.9 m/s; mass ratio 1.154; capacity 443.5 m/s. Budget 234.85 m/s; with margin 293.56 m/s → 813.4 kg, 67.8% of the propellant.

S-C4INTEGRATE

Numerical propagation step error

Different integrators fail in different ways. Measure, do not assume. This result justifies the integrator and step you chose.

εr = |rN| − r0 · εE = (EN − E0)/|E0| · εx = |xN − xexact|

Propagate the 420 km circular orbit for 558 steps of Δt = 10 s (5,580 s) with explicit Euler, semi-implicit Euler, velocity Verlet and RK4. Plot all four in the GDD.

Ref: Euler εr +925.0 km, εE +0.111; semi-implicit −62.7 m, εx 3.84 km; Verlet ≈ 0 m, εx 1.81 km (phase); RK4 −0.2 mm, εx 1.7 cm.

S-C5PRECISION

Floating-point resolution and floating origin

A 32-bit float has 24 bits of mantissa. At orbital radius, that is too coarse to dock. Prove it, then show how your architecture avoids it.

ULP(x) = 2⌊log₂|x|⌋ − 23 (float32) · 2⌊log₂|x|⌋ − 52 (float64)

At r = 6,798 km and at 1,000 m from a floating origin. Compare with the 0.10 m docking tolerance.

Ref: float32 at 6.798 × 10⁶ m: 0.5 m (fails); float64: 9.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m; float32 at 1,000 m: 6.1 × 10⁻⁵ m.

S-C6CW

Clohessy–Wiltshire drift

In the station's frame, being lower means drifting ahead. This is the core counter-intuition of proximity operations, and your predicted-path display must show it.

ẍ − 3n²x − 2nẏ = 0 · ÿ + 2nẋ = 0 · Δyorbit = 3π·Δx · ẏ = −(3/2)n·x

x radial (up), y along-track. For a ferry 1.0 km below the station on a circular orbit.

Ref: along-track drift 9.42 km per orbit; relative speed 1.69 m/s ahead.

S-C7DOCK

Docking contact energy

The docking mechanism absorbs relative kinetic energy, which depends on the reduced mass of the two vehicles. This sets the true speed limit.

mr = m₁m₂ / (m₁ + m₂) · E = ½mrv² · vmax = √(2Edock/mr)

Kestrel mass after the S-C2 transfer; Aurora 180,000 kg; closing at 0.05 m/s.

Ref: m₁ = 8,638.7 kg; mr = 8,243.1 kg; E = 10.30 J; vmax = 0.0853 m/s, so the 0.08 m/s limit keeps a 12% energy margin.

S-C8MASS

Centre-of-mass shift and disturbance torque

Shared tanks drain unevenly. An off-axis centre of mass turns every OMS burn into a torque that the RCS must fight.

Δycm = d·(mR − mL) / M · τ = TOMS·Δycm · duty = τ / (TRCS·ℓ)

A 40 kg imbalance between tanks at ±0.60 m, at the S-C7 mass.

Ref: Δycm = 2.78 mm; τ = 22.2 N·m; one RCS thruster gives 836 N·m, so a 2.66% duty cycle holds attitude.

S-C9ATMOS

Layered atmosphere density

Density is the input to both drag and heating. Compute one point by hand to prove your atmosphere module.

h′ = r0h/(r0+h) · T = Tb + L(h′ − hb) · P = Pb(Tb/T)g₀M/(R*L) · ρ = P/(RT)

At geometric altitude 60.0 km.

Ref: h′ = 59,439 m (layer 5); T = 247.02 K; exponent −12.2009; P = 21.96 Pa; ρ = 3.097 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m³.

S-C10AERO

Drag polar, lift and deceleration

Bank angle rotates the lift vector: its vertical part controls how fast the capsule descends, its horizontal part steers. This is the player's only control in P4.

q = ½ρv² · D = qCDA · L = qCLA · Lvert = L·cosσ · n = √(D² + L²)/(m·g0)

At 60.0 km, v = 6,600 m/s, m = 8,000 kg, σ = 60°.

Ref: q = 6,745 Pa; D = 104.7 kN; L = 31.4 kN; Lvert = 15.7 kN; drag deceleration 1.34 g; total aerodynamic load 1.39 g.

S-C11HEAT

Stagnation heat flux and wall temperature

Heat flux grows with the cube of speed. The radiative-equilibrium temperature tells your shader how hot the shield should glow.

q̇ = k√(ρ/Rn)·v³ · Tw = (q̇ / εσ)¼

At the S-C10 point; then at the peak of the reference σ = 60° trajectory.

Ref: 429.9 kW/m², Tw 1,728 K. Reference peak: 443.7 kW/m² at 59.7 km, 6,634 m/s; Tw 1,742 K.

S-C12LOAD

Heat load and the entry corridor

Lift-up keeps the g-load low but stays hot for longer; diving cuts the heat load but crushes the crew. The corridor is where both limits hold.

Q = ∫q̇ dt (trapezoidal, EI → 10 km) · margin = 1 − Q/Qmax

Integrate your own game's trajectory at constant σ = 0°, 60° and 90°, planar, RK4 at Δt ≤ 0.05 s, and compare with the reference.

Ref (Q, peak q̇, peak n): σ 0°: 177.2 MJ/m² (over budget), 344 kW/m², 2.49 g. σ 45°: 157.8, 380, 3.37 g. σ 60°: 144.6, 444, 4.21 g (12.4% margin). σ 75°: 130.8, 521, 5.83 g, above 5 g for 49.9 s (fails). σ 90°: 118.9, 602 (over the flux limit), 8.81 g (fails).

S-C13SYSTEMS

Power, thermal and life-support balance

The station orbit spends over a third of its time in Earth's shadow. The battery, the radiator and the oxygen supply must all close the loop.

fecl = asin(RE/r)/π · Eecl = Ploadtecl · Q̇rad = εσT⁴A · mO₂ = n·ṁ·t

At 420 km with the Sun in the orbit plane; recharge with η = 0.90; heat = load + crew metabolic.

Ref: eclipse 38.75%, 36.03 min; Eecl 1.561 kWh (≤ 4.0 kWh usable); surplus 2.088 kWh vs 1.735 needed, 20.4% margin. Radiator 4.09 kW vs 2.9 kW. O₂ 7.56 kg.

S-C14PROFILE

Telemetry memory, texture memory and warp cost

Know your memory before the profiler tells you. Pre-allocate what you can predict, compress what you can, and count the physics steps that time warp demands.

B = channels × 8 B × f × t · tex = w × h × bpp × 4/3 (mips) · steps/frame = warp / (Δt·fdisplay)

32 channels, float64, 50 Hz, over a 30-minute session and a 120 s ring buffer; CSV at about 13 bytes per value; one 4096² texture; ×1000 warp with RK4 at Δt = 1 s.

Ref: 23.04 MB session; 1.536 MB ring buffer; CSV ≈ 37.4 MB. RGBA8 64 MiB (85.3 MiB with mips); BC7 16 MiB (21.3 MiB). ×1000 warp: 16.7 RK4 steps per 60 Hz frame.

4.1Why there is no fill-in template

A loop is not a list of boxes

A core loop is a cycle of decisions, feedback and consequences, and its quality lies in how the parts pull on each other. A template with a box for "core loop" invites a sentence; the structure below asks you to prove the loop works, with diagrams, playtest data and numbers.

State is the design

What happens when the player pauses mid-burn, restarts during a parachute deployment or unplugs the gamepad on the results screen is a design decision encoded in a state machine. No template can know your states; reviewers reward documents whose diagrams match the code exactly.

Physics pipelines are bespoke

A Junior lander on semi-implicit Euler and a Senior capsule on RK4 with a layered atmosphere are both legitimate and judged on the same criteria. What matters is the chain from model to derivation to code to measured behaviour, and that cannot be forced into a blank.

The division challenges, fact sheets and performance envelope in Sections 01 and 03, and the GDD and package specifications in Section 04, form part of these rules. Where the wording of a rule is unclear, the interpretation of the organisers under R-12 applies.     R-01

    Eligibility & teams

    1. 1.1
      Open to students enrolled in Grades 6–12 at any recognised school in India for the 2026–27 academic year. Junior division: Grades 6–8. Senior division: Grades 9–12.
    2. 1.2
      A team has 2–4 students from the same school and one faculty advisor who is a teacher at that school. A team with members in both grade bands competes in the division of its most senior member.
    3. 1.3

    4. 1.4

    5. 1.5

    2. 2.2

    3. 2.3
      All official communication goes through the portal or the registered advisor's email. Teams must not contact reviewers or jurors about the competition before results are published.
    4. 2.4

    R-05

    Originality, asset licensing & AI governance

    Read in full before you start · applies to Round 1 and Round 2

    Vector GameJam tests your studio's own design and engineering judgement. The review board and the jury must be able to trust that the code, shaders, visual scripts, levels, art, audio, words and numbers in your game, source, GDD and presentation are yours, and that everything that is not yours is declared and licensed.

    This rule is deliberately specific so that no team is unsure what is allowed. If a use of a tool or asset is not clearly listed as allowed below, treat it as not allowed, or ask the organisers in writing before the deadline.

    5.1 What counts as "AI"

    For this competition, AI means any software that generates, rewrites, completes, translates, summarises, designs, optimises or suggests content (code, shaders, visual-script graphs, text, numbers, levels, images, textures, 3D models, animation, audio, music, voice or slides) instead of simply recording, compiling, displaying or calculating exactly what you typed, drew, modelled or entered. This includes AI built into editors, IDEs, engines and creative tools, whether or not the feature is labelled "AI".

    It counts as using AI whether you typed a prompt, accepted an inline code suggestion, pressed a button such as "Generate", "Generative fill", "Create with AI", "Explain and fix", "Magic" or "Auto-complete function", or let a tool create or reshape code, art or sound for you. Ordinary symbol completion that lists the names of existing functions and variables is not AI for this rule. Not knowing that a feature uses AI is not a defence.

    5.3 The three-tier integrity model

    Permitted standard tools

    Ordinary tools that compile, edit, draw, record or check your own work. No declaration needed beyond listing software and version in §7.4.

    Tier 2
    Permitted with mandatory disclosure

    Limited, declared help. Every use must be recorded in the §7.4 ledger: tool or source, date, what you asked or took, what you did with it, and where it is used.

    • Pre-existing open art and sound assets under CC0 or CC-BY, with explicit attribution in §7.4, in the credits and in ASSETS.csv, used for non-core content such as ambient sound, fonts or background textures. The Art, Audio & UI criterion credits only original work.
    • AI troubleshooting for syntax and bug fixing: asking an AI to explain an error message or why a line your team wrote fails. Any fix must be typed by the team, must be a small change to existing code, and must be logged with its commit hash. You may not paste AI-written code.
    • Conceptual algorithm research: asking an AI to explain a concept such as RK4, a quaternion or a state machine, which you then study and implement from a cited primary source.
    • Engine documentation assistants that point you to official documentation, provided you read and cite the documentation itself.
    Tier 3
    Strictly prohibited

    Any use in which AI or a third party produces, designs or decides part of your game, source, document or presentation.

    • AI writing game code: scripts, mechanics, physics, shaders, visual-script graphs, tests or tools, in whole or in part, including accepted inline completions.
    • AI generating content: full 3D models, environments, sprites, textures, animation, levels, music, sound effects or voice.
    • Text-to-game generators and prompt-to-prototype tools of any kind.
    • Pre-made projects: template projects, sample scenes, starter kits, tutorial projects or commercial asset packs submitted as, or used as the base of, the game, with or without reskinning.
    • AI writing or calculating: generating, rewriting, paraphrasing or translating GDD text, or solving the mandatory calculations.
    • AI-generated presentation slides, and any AI tool during the Round 2 pitch, scrutineering or patch sprint.

    5.5 Audit and verification protocol

    1. Build reproduction. Reviewers may rebuild any finalist's game from the submitted source archive and compare it with the submitted build. A build that cannot be reproduced must be explained.

    5. Writing and code-style checks. Reviewers use their judgement, comparison with live answers and the version history. Automated AI detectors are not used as sole evidence.
    6. Right to respond. A team suspected of a breach is told what the concern is and may respond, with its advisor, before any decision is made.

    6.1Technical game development FAQ

    Q01

    Should we build in 2D or 3D?

    Build in the dimension that serves the physics and that your team can finish. Junior studios almost always do better in 2D: a side-view lander shows thrust, gravity and drag with perfect clarity, and every hour not spent on 3D cameras and lighting goes into feel and balance. Senior studios need 3D or 2.5D for attitude, docking alignment and the entry corridor, but a planar orbit with a 3D camera is a legitimate choice for P1 if it is declared.

    Reviewers do not award extra marks for dimensions. A clean, stable 2D game with a true flight model beats an unstable 3D one every time.

    Q02

    What are the limits for web builds, and why does WebGL run out of memory?

    CelesteCon's limits are in §4.3: Junior web builds ≤ 150 MB and Senior ≤ 300 MB as zipped, and every build must run within 1 GB of browser memory (Junior) or 3 GB of process memory (Senior). Hosting platforms such as itch.io apply their own limits on file size and file count, so check the platform's current documentation before your final week, not on deadline day.

    Web exports run inside a WebAssembly heap that is allocated in advance and cannot exceed 4 GB in 32-bit WebAssembly; in practice browsers often refuse much less, especially on machines with other tabs open. Most "out of memory" failures come from uncompressed textures, audio decoded in full at load, and every level held in memory at once. Compress textures, stream music, load levels on demand, and watch memory in the browser's developer tools across three restarts. If it climbs, you have a leak.

    Q03

    Can we use third-party sound effects, music or audio middleware?

    Audio middleware and engine audio systems are tools (Tier 1), provided their licence allows your use and they are listed in ASSETS.csv. Pre-existing sound effects and music under CC0 or CC-BY are Tier 2: permitted with attribution in the credits, in §7.4 and in the asset register, and only for non-core content. The Art, Audio & UI criterion credits original work, so the engine note, warnings and feedback sounds that carry information should be made or recorded by your team.

    Assets whose licence forbids redistribution, requires a purchase you have not made, or is unclear may not be used. AI-generated music, sound effects and voices are Tier 3 and prohibited.

    Q04

    How do we make physics frame-rate independent with delta time?

    Do not multiply your physics by the frame's delta and call it done: a variable step still changes the results at different frame rates (J-C6 shows why). Instead, add each frame's elapsed time to an accumulator and run as many fixed steps as fit: acc += frameTime; while (acc >= DT) { step(DT); acc -= DT; }. Clamp frameTime (or the number of steps) so that a long stall cannot trigger hundreds of catch-up steps, the "spiral of death". Then draw the scene interpolated between the previous and current physics states using alpha = acc / DT.

    Most engines offer a fixed-update callback (for example Unity's FixedUpdate or Godot's _physics_process); you may use it, but you must set its rate to the fact-sheet value, apply your own forces inside it, and be able to explain how the engine accumulates time. Custom engines must implement the accumulator themselves.

    Q05

    What hardware will the kiosks at DPS R.K. Puram have?

    The kiosk reference specification is: Windows 11 x64; a six-core desktop processor of Intel Core i5 twelfth-generation or AMD Ryzen 5 5600 class; 16 GB RAM; a discrete graphics card of NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1650 class with 4 GB of video memory; a 24-inch 1920 × 1080 display at 60 Hz; stereo headphones; a USB keyboard and mouse; one Xbox-layout XInput gamepad; and current Chrome and Firefox. The kiosks have no internet connection. The final specification is confirmed to shortlisted studios with the schedule.

    Test on the weakest machine you can find, and test Junior builds on integrated graphics as well. A game that holds 60 FPS on a school laptop will hold it on the kiosk.

    Q06

    Controller or keyboard: how should we handle input mapping?

    Both, through one layer. Map physical inputs to named actions (throttle up, rotate left, deploy chute, RCS translate forward) and have gameplay code read only the actions. That makes remapping, gamepad support and the one-switch curveball simple. Analogue sticks and triggers should drive analogue values such as throttle and rotation rate, with a small dead zone; keys should ramp those values at a stated rate so that keyboard players are not disadvantaged.

    Sample input once per frame and hand the result to every fixed step that runs in that frame, so that a tap is never lost when two physics steps run, nor applied twice. Show the current bindings in the pause menu, and handle a gamepad disconnect by pausing.

    Q07

    Can we use the engine's built-in physics?

    Junior: yes for collision detection and contact response, but the flight forces (thrust, gravity, drag, parachute) must be computed and applied by your own code every fixed step, and your calculations must show that the engine is doing what you expect (J-C9). Senior: engine physics may be used only for docking contact. Orbital propagation, relative motion, atmosphere, aerodynamics and heating must be team-written, because that is what the Senior challenge is testing.

    Q08

    Can Junior teams use Scratch?

    Yes, if the project is packaged as a web or desktop build that meets R-04 and uses an architecture a reviewer can follow: custom blocks for the physics step and the state machine, lists for parameters, and a timer-driven fixed step rather than "forever" loops that run at whatever speed the computer allows. The GDD must include screenshots of the key scripts in place of code listings. Scratch entries are judged on the same rubric; ambition within the tool is rewarded.

    Q09

    Do Unreal Blueprints or other visual scripts count as code?

    Yes. Blueprints, Godot visual scripts, Shader Graph and similar node graphs are code for every purpose of these rules: they must be written by the team, appear in the GDD as captioned figures with the asset path and commit hash, and be explained live in scrutineering. Keep graphs tidy with comments and functions; jurors will open them. Senior teams using Shader Graph for the mandatory shader must be able to explain every node's mathematics.

    Q10

    How long should the game be?

    Long enough to contain the challenge, short enough to polish. As a guide, a skilled player should finish a Junior game in 8–15 minutes and a Senior game in 20–40 minutes of play with time warp. Remember that a juror playtest lasts 10 minutes and begins with a cold start, so the first two minutes matter more than the last twenty. Include a level or phase select after first completion so that jurors can reach any part of the game quickly.

    Q11

    Can we keep developing after Round 1?

    Yes. The Round 1 build and source are frozen as the judged record (R-03.6), but you may keep improving the game in the same repository. List every change in the one-page change log with your kiosk build (R-06.4); jurors will ask what each change did and why. Undeclared changes are penalised under R-07.

    Q12

    What does double-blind mean for our credits, commits and hosted page?

    Reviewers must not be able to tell who you are or where you are from. In the game credits, list roles under your in-game studio name and code, and list third-party assets in full. Set commit author names to role names from the start (R-02.4). A hosted mirror must be unlisted and must not show a personal or school account name. After results, you are free to publish the game under your real names.

    Q13

    Can a Junior team go further and use Senior methods?

    Yes. Junior teams may write RK4 integrators, add orbital phases or build shaders, and reviewers will credit correct work. You are still judged against the Junior brief and rubric, and J-C1 to J-C10 remain mandatory. Deliver the Junior requirements well first.

    Q14

    Can we use an AI assistant to learn or to fix bugs?

    You may ask an AI tool to explain a concept, or to explain an error message or why a line your team wrote fails, and you must log every such use in §7.4 (Tier 2). You may not paste code, shaders, graphs, text or assets that an AI produced, and you must switch off AI code completion in your editor (Tier 3). The rule of thumb: if the AI's output could be pasted into your project, you are not allowed to use it that way. AI tools and internet access are not available during Round 2.

    Q15

    What should we bring on expo day?

    One or two development laptops per team with the engine, the exact engine version and the project installed and building offline; chargers and an extension board; the kiosk build on two USB drives; offline copies of the engine documentation you rely on; your printed GDD; and any declared controller or joystick. Test a full offline build the night before: the sprint network is off, and package downloads will fail.

    Q16

    Is Vector GameJam affiliated with any engine maker or platform?

    No. Vector GameJam is an independent CelesteCon competition organised by AEROSS. Engines, tools and platforms are named on this page only to describe what teams may use. No affiliation, sponsorship or endorsement is implied unless CelesteCon formally announces a partnership, and every permitted engine is judged on equal terms.
